**Format mirrors the real final:** Section I True/False · Section II reading-based short answer · Section III a short essay.

**How to use it:** try it closed-book first, then check the answer keys and model answers that follow each section.

# SECTION I — TRUE / FALSE (22 × 2 = 44 marks)

> Write **T** or **F**. Each statement tests one precise fact or claim — the false ones usually swap two real concepts, so read carefully.

1. A *reserve* is the part of a resource that is both identified and economically recoverable under current conditions.
2. Natural uranium is about 7% U-235, which is why it must still be enriched before it can be used as reactor fuel.
3. The Air Quality Index (AQI) is calculated by taking the **average** of the sub-indices of the criteria pollutants.
4. Ostrom argued that what Hardin called the "tragedy of the commons" really describes *open-access* resources, and that *common-property* regimes with rules can avoid that tragedy.
5. A carbon tax fixes the **quantity** of emissions and lets the market set the price.
6. The Coase theorem holds that bargaining can resolve an externality efficiently only when property rights are clear and transaction costs are negligible.
7. Methylmercury biomagnifies, so large, long-lived predatory fish carry higher concentrations than small fish.
8. "Global dimming" describes a net **warming** effect, because aerosols trap heat close to the surface.
9. Under the dolphin-safe regime, ending the practice of setting nets on dolphins eliminated the bycatch of all species.
10. The Basel Convention bans exports of hazardous waste from rich to poor countries, and the United States has ratified it.
11. The oceans hold roughly 97.5% of the world's water, leaving fresh liquid water as a small fraction of the remainder.
12. The rate-determining (slowest) step in acid mine drainage is the oxidation of Fe²⁺ to Fe³⁺.
13. In the United States, bottled water is regulated by the EPA under the Safe Drinking Water Act, making it more tightly controlled than tap water.
14. The Clean Water Act (1972) governs the quality of surface-water bodies, whereas the Safe Drinking Water Act (1974) governs drinking water.
15. The IPAT identity expresses environmental impact as the product of Population, Affluence and Technology.
16. Boserup's "induced intensification" holds that population growth inevitably outstrips food supply and leads to collapse.
17. A "charismatic species" is one that easily attracts public sympathy and protection, such as the dolphin.
18. The Jevons paradox states that improving the efficiency of a resource's use can *increase* rather than decrease its total consumption.
19. In the United States, processed-potato consumption overtook fresh-potato consumption around 1970.
20. Pure gold is chemically toxic, which is the direct reason gold mining poisons the people who work it.
21. An Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ) gives a coastal state resource jurisdiction out to 200 nautical miles, while the high seas beyond remain an open commons.
22. Eutrophication is driven by an excess of nutrients such as nitrogen and phosphorus entering a body of water.

## Section I — Answer key & explanations

1. **T** — identified *and* economically recoverable now; that is exactly what separates a reserve from the broader resource.
2. **F** — natural uranium is **0.7%** U-235. Reactor fuel needs **> 4%**; a weapon needs **~7%**.
3. **F** — the AQI takes the **maximum** sub-index, not the average, so the single worst pollutant sets the reported value.
4. **T** — this is Ostrom's central correction of Hardin: open access ≠ common property; well-designed institutions can sustain a commons.
5. **F** — a **carbon tax fixes the price** and lets quantity vary; it is **cap-and-trade** that fixes the quantity (the cap) and lets the price vary.
6. **T** — clear property rights + negligible transaction costs are the two preconditions of the Coase theorem.
7. **T** — biomagnification up the food chain is why advisories target large predatory fish.
8. **F** — it is a net **cooling** effect: aerosols reflect incoming sunlight and thicken clouds.
9. **F** — switching to log sets produced **2–3× more bycatch** of other species (sharks, turtles, juvenile fish); it relocated the harm rather than removing it.
10. **F** — the U.S. **signed but never ratified** the Basel Convention.
11. **T** — ~97.5% is ocean; fresh water is only ~0.64% of the total, and 99% of *that* is groundwater.
12. **T** — the slow Fe²⁺→Fe³⁺ oxidation (half-life ~1000 days at pH < 3) governs the overall rate.
13. **F** — bottled water is regulated by the **FDA** (more loosely); **tap** water is the EPA's domain under the SDWA.
14. **T** — CWA = water bodies ("fishable and swimmable"); SDWA = drinking water (MCLs).
15. **T** — Impact = P × A × T.
16. **F** — that is **Malthus**. Boserup argued the opposite: population pressure **induces** agricultural innovation and intensification.
17. **T** — charismatic species win disproportionate protection; the dolphin is the textbook case.
18. **T** — efficiency lowers effective cost and can raise total use (the rebound effect).
19. **T** — around 1970 processed (mainly frozen fries) overtook fresh consumption — the turn toward an industrial diet.
20. **F** — gold itself is **non-toxic**; the poisoning comes from the **mercury** used in amalgamation and the **arsenic** associated with gold ore.
21. **T** — 200 nm EEZ for the coastal state; beyond it the high seas are an open commons (hence the race to harvest).
22. **T** — nutrient over-enrichment (N and P) drives algal blooms, oxygen depletion and dead zones.

# SECTION II — READING-BASED SHORT ANSWER (3 passages × ~11 marks = 33 marks)

> Each passage below is a short piece of "frontier literature." Read it, then answer the questions in **2–5 sentences** each, **using the course concepts where relevant**. Model answers follow the whole section.

## Passage A — "Why the world keeps finding more metal" (natural-science / resource economics)

> It is tempting to think of a metal like copper as a fixed quantity buried in the crust, steadily drawn down toward exhaustion. The accounting tells a stranger story. Over the past century humanity has mined enormous tonnages of copper, yet the *reserves* booked on the world's balance sheet today are several times larger than they were generations ago. The paradox dissolves once we separate two ideas. The total *resource* is everything in the crust, most of it unmapped and much of it too dilute or too deep to extract at a profit. A *reserve* is the narrow slice of that resource we have actually located **and** can mine economically right now. Reserves therefore expand or contract not only when rock is dug up, but whenever the metal's price rises, a new deposit is confirmed, or extraction technology improves — each of these pulls more of the vast resource across the line into the recoverable category. Two cautions follow. First, a deposit's value is fixed to one place: unlike a factory, a mine cannot be relocated to cheaper ground, so its costs are hostage to local geology, labour and politics. Second, turning a promising target into a booked reserve always carries discovery risk — most prospects never prove out, and the spending happens long before any return is certain.

**A1.** Using the passage, explain the difference between a *resource* and a *reserve*, and why total reserves can grow even while mining continues. *(4 marks)*
**A2.** The passage says a mineral deposit's value is "fixed to one place." Name one economic or environmental-justice consequence that follows from this fixed location. *(3 marks)*
**A3.** How does the resource-vs-reserve distinction complicate simple "we are running out" (neo-Malthusian) predictions about minerals? Refer to a relevant idea from the course. *(4 marks)*

## Passage B — "Who pays for a tonne of carbon?" (political economy / critical social science)

> A carbon offset promises something elegant: a polluter in a wealthy country pays for an equivalent reduction somewhere cheaper, and the atmosphere, indifferent to geography, comes out even. On paper the molecules balance. On the ground the balance can look very different. Consider a tree-planting offset in which a Northern firm acquires a large tract of land in the global South to grow a carbon-absorbing plantation. The accounting credits the project with the carbon its trees will eventually store; what the accounting omits is who used that land before, on what terms they lost access to it, and how many of the promised local jobs ever materialise. In more than one documented case, communities that had farmed or grazed an area for generations found themselves fenced out so that a fraction of the acquired land could be planted, while only a handful of wage jobs appeared in return. The deeper point is that an offset does not reduce the consuming society's appetite for energy; it allows that appetite to continue while relocating the obligation to act. "Green consumption," in this reading, is still consumption — and because the costs and benefits of the global carbon economy are distributed so unevenly, a transaction that looks costless from the buyer's side may be quite costly from the seller's.

**B1.** What does the passage mean by saying that in an offset "the molecules balance" but "the balance on the ground can look very different"? *(4 marks)*
**B2.** Identify **two** social or distributional costs the passage says carbon accounting tends to leave out. *(3 marks)*
**B3.** The passage claims "green consumption is still consumption." Connect this to the **political-economy** critique of market-based environmental solutions, naming one concept from the course. *(4 marks)*

## Passage C — "A label that saved one species and strained another" (ethics / markets / social construction)

> Few environmental campaigns succeeded as quickly as the one that put a "dolphin-safe" stamp on cans of tuna. Public outrage at images of dolphins drowning in tuna nets translated, within a few years, into commitments from the brands that controlled most of the market, and dolphin deaths in the fishery fell dramatically. By the usual measure — did the targeted harm go down? — it was a triumph of conscientious consumption. Yet marine scientists watching the wider ecosystem grew uneasy. To avoid setting nets where dolphins swim, many boats shifted to other methods, such as concentrating gear around floating objects that attract fish. These methods spared dolphins but swept up far more of everything else — sharks, sea turtles, and the juveniles of the tuna species themselves — at rates well above the old technique. Some fleets simply moved into waters where the rules did not reach, hastening the depletion of stocks there. The episode raises an uncomfortable question for market environmentalism: a certification scheme can only protect what consumers have been taught to see. The dolphin was a charismatic animal whose suffering was legible to shoppers; the shark, the turtle and the unseen collapse of a fishery were not. The label, in other words, solved the problem people were watching and displaced the one they were not.

**C1.** Explain the central paradox the passage describes about the dolphin-safe label. *(4 marks)*
**C2.** The passage calls the dolphin "a charismatic animal." Define a **charismatic species** and explain how the idea connects to the **social construction of nature**. *(3 marks)*
**C3.** Use this case to state one strength **and** one limitation of market-based environmental tools (eco-labels/green certification). *(4 marks)*

## Section II — Model answers

**A1.** The *resource* is the entire amount of a metal in the crust, including the deep, dilute and unmapped material that cannot currently be mined at a profit. A *reserve* is the much smaller portion that has been both located and shown to be economically recoverable under present conditions. Because the line between them is set by price, discovery and technology — not just by how much rock has been dug — reserves can grow even as mining continues: a higher price or a better extraction method pulls previously "sub-economic" resource across the line into the reserve category. (This is the copper paradox: reserves today are several times larger than a century ago despite heavy mining.)

**A2.** Because a deposit cannot be moved, its costs are tied to the specific geology, labour conditions and politics of that one location. Consequences include: the mine's profitability is hostage to local factors (economic), and — for environmental justice — the pollution, health burden and land disturbance fall on whoever lives at that fixed site (e.g. the Navajo and uranium, or AMD-poisoned watersheds), since the harm cannot be relocated to those who consume the metal.

**A3.** Neo-Malthusian predictions treat minerals as a fixed stock being drawn down to exhaustion, implying inevitable scarcity. The resource-vs-reserve distinction shows that "how much is left" is not fixed but responds to price, technology and discovery — exactly the logic of the **market response model**, in which scarcity raises price, which then induces more supply, substitution and innovation. So crude "we are running out" forecasts can be wrong because the recoverable quantity is partly an economic, not purely a geological, variable. (The honest qualifier: this does not abolish physical limits or the rising environmental cost of reaching lower-grade ore.)

**B1.** Because the atmosphere is global, a tonne of CO₂ avoided in the global South genuinely offsets a tonne emitted in the North — so in pure carbon accounting ("the molecules") the deal nets to zero. But that accounting ignores the *social* terms of the exchange on the ground: who previously used the land, how they lost access, and whether the promised benefits arrived. So a transaction that is carbon-neutral on paper can impose real, uncompensated losses on the people where the offset is sited.

**B2.** Any two of: (i) the prior users/inhabitants who are fenced out or lose access to land they farmed or grazed (displacement / loss of the commons); (ii) the shortfall between promised and actual local jobs or benefits; (iii) the fact that the consuming society's energy demand is not reduced at all — the obligation to change is simply relocated rather than met.

**B3.** The political-economy critique argues that market fixes leave the underlying driver — endless **capital accumulation** and rising consumption — untouched, and merely move the problem around through a **spatial fix** (relocating the burden to cheaper places). "Green consumption is still consumption" captures this: an offset lets affluent consumption continue while exporting the cost to poorer regions under conditions of **uneven development**, so wealth is rearranged but emissions (and injustice) are not actually resolved. (Accept also: environmental justice, manufactured demand, greenwashing.)

**C1.** The paradox is that the label was a clear success by its own narrow metric — dolphin deaths fell sharply — yet it may have produced a wider ecological *defeat*. Avoiding dolphins pushed fleets toward methods (e.g. fish-aggregating sets) that killed far more sharks, turtles and juvenile tuna, or pushed fishing into unregulated waters, so the total ecological harm may have risen even as the visible, targeted harm fell.

**C2.** A **charismatic species** is one that easily attracts public affection and therefore disproportionate protection (the dolphin, the panda). The link to the **social construction of nature** is that which harms "count" is shaped by how society is taught to see them: the dolphin's suffering was made legible and emotionally vivid to shoppers, while the shark, turtle and the slow collapse of a fishery were not — so the campaign protected the constructed object of concern, not necessarily the ecosystem.

**C3.** *Strength:* eco-labels harness consumer pressure to change powerful firms quickly without waiting for legislation — the dolphin-safe label moved ~90% of the market and cut dolphin mortality ~99% in a few years. *Limitation:* a label can only protect what consumers are taught to see, so it can displace harm onto unmonitored species or places and create a false sense that the problem is solved (a greenwashing risk); certification schemes (e.g. MSC) can also raise costs that exclude small producers. (Accept the WTO 2012 ruling that the standard was misleading as further evidence of the limitation.)

# SECTION III — SHORT ESSAY (1 essay × 23 marks)

> Choose **ONE** prompt. Write roughly 500–700 words. You are graded on (a) correct natural-science mechanism, (b) genuine use of **at least three lenses**, and (c) critical evaluation — naming the limits of any "solution." A model structure for each follows.

**Prompt 1 (the signature multi-lens essay).** Choose **one** environmental object from the course — carbon dioxide, uranium/nuclear power, e-waste, bottled water, tuna, or the french fry — and analyse it through **at least three** of the seven lenses. Explain the relevant science first, then show how each lens frames the problem and what it proposes, and end by assessing which lens you find most persuasive and why.

**Prompt 2 (the critical-evaluation essay).** "Market-based tools — carbon taxes, cap-and-trade, eco-labels, the privatisation of a commons — are the most realistic way to solve environmental problems." Evaluate this claim. Use at least **one** market success and **one** documented failure or critique from the course, and bring in at least **two** lenses (e.g. markets vs political economy, or markets vs institutions).

## Model structure for Prompt 1 — worked example: **carbon dioxide**

*(Use this as a template; the same skeleton works for any object — swap in its science and its three lenses.)*

**Opening (frame + thesis).** State why the object matters and preview your three lenses. *e.g.* "CO₂ is the defining commons problem of the Anthropocene; I analyse it through the institutions, market and political-economy lenses, and argue the first two are necessary but only the third explains why solutions keep stalling."

**Paragraph 1 — the science (do this first, always).** The greenhouse effect is natural and necessary; the problem is the *enhanced* effect from fossil-fuel CO₂. Anchor it with data: CO₂ ≈ 428 ppm (2026), already +1.1 °C; IPCC AR6 calls human influence "unequivocal"; the remaining carbon budget (~2900 Gt, ~65% used) means 1.5 °C requires net zero. This shows you can separate mechanism from politics.

**Paragraph 2 — Lens A: Institutions / the commons.** The atmosphere is the largest open-access commons, so each country free-rides → a prisoner's dilemma. The institutional response is international agreement: Kyoto (1997, binding, developed countries only) and Paris (2015, universal but voluntary NDCs). *Critique:* enforcement is weak and the free-rider problem persists — institutions are necessary but not self-sufficient.

**Paragraph 3 — Lens B: Markets / economics.** CO₂ is a textbook **externality**; the market response is to price it. Compare a **carbon tax** (predictable price, economy-wide, revenue-neutral) with **cap-and-trade** (fixes the quantity but historical allocation creates windfalls and may cover only part of the economy). Cite a real scheme (e.g. the Shenzhen ETS). *Critique:* pricing can become **greenwashing** or rely on offsets that do not truly offset.

**Paragraph 4 — Lens C: Political economy.** The deeper driver is **capital accumulation**: production expands regardless of ecological limits (the 2nd contradiction of capitalism), and market fixes can relocate rather than reduce emissions via a **spatial fix**, under conditions of **uneven development** (U.S. per-capita emissions ~5× India's; historical responsibility). This lens explains *why* the institutional and market fixes underperform, and favours regulation plus equity.

**Conclusion (judgement).** Don't sit on the fence — argue for one, while acknowledging the others. *e.g.* "Pricing and treaties are indispensable, but without the political-economy lens we mistake a structural problem for a technical one; the most persuasive account combines a carbon tax with binding institutions *and* a just distribution of the burden." End on the course's signature note: every 'solution' carries its own critique — that scepticism is the point.

## Model skeleton for Prompt 2

- **Thesis:** market tools are powerful but incomplete; they work best as one instrument inside institutional and regulatory frameworks, not as a standalone fix.
- **Concede the strength (markets lens):** a real success — the **dolphin-safe label** moved ~90% of the market and cut dolphin mortality ~99%; or a **carbon tax's** efficiency and predictability; or how markets turn a waste externality into a recoverable commodity (e-waste R2/e-Steward).
- **Show the failure/critique (pick the contrast):** the dolphin-safe label displaced bycatch onto other species (a rights victory, an ecological defeat; WTO 2012 ruled it misleading); **bottled water** as **primitive accumulation** of a free commons sold back at ~250× the carbon footprint and >1000× the price, with the FDA/EPA regulatory asymmetry; the **CRT market collapse** showing markets abandon waste the moment it stops being profitable, so regulation (California DTSC) must backstop it.
- **Second lens (institutions or political economy):** Ostrom shows well-designed **institutions** can govern a commons without full privatisation; the **political-economy** lens shows market fixes can relocate harm (spatial fix) and entrench **environmental injustice** rather than resolve it.
- **Judgement:** markets are necessary but not sufficient; the question mark in "Environmental *Solution?*" applies to every market tool — name what each one hides.
