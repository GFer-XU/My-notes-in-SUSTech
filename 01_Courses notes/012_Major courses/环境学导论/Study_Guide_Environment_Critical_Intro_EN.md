# PART 1 — THE CORE FRAMEWORK: 7 ANALYTICAL "LENSES"

RHM teaches **7 ways of seeing an environmental problem (lenses)**, then applies them to 9 concrete **"objects"** (carbon, uranium, tuna, bottled water, e-waste, etc.). Each lens gives a *different* answer to "What is the root of the problem, and how do we solve it?" **This is the highest-frequency exam material.**

## Lens 0 — The Anthropocene & the "human-made wilderness" (foundational concept)

- **Anthropocene** — a metaphor for the present era: humans exert enormous influence on the global environment, yet cannot truly *control* these extremely complex ecological systems.
- **Core thesis (very important):** Environment and society *cannot be separated*. Humans are environmental beings governed by organic processes; environmental processes are fundamentally social (e.g., photosynthesis is the basis of agriculture). The Dutch **Oostvaardersplassen** "human-made wilderness" shows this: a seemingly "natural" reserve was entirely designed by people and built on reclaimed land — **"wilderness" itself is socially constructed.** Yellowstone, too, was demarcated only after expelling Native peoples.
- **Rewilding** — deliberately restoring or creating ecological functions believed to have existed before human influence (often requires reintroducing large predators).
- **Takeaway:** Environmental decisions cannot rest on "this is natural / this is social," because the two are inseparably interwoven.

## Lens 1 — Population

**Core claim:** Population growth is the primary driver of environmental degradation.

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Malthus** | Population grows *geometrically* (exponentially); food grows *arithmetically* → scarcity is inevitable. "Positive checks" (famine, plague, war) and "preventive checks" (moral restraint / late marriage) hold population down. |
| **Critiques of Malthus** | ① ignores affluence and technology; ② excuses poverty (lets economic/political structures and elites off the hook); ③ his views on women are biased. |
| **Neo-Malthusians** | Modern heirs (e.g., Paul Ehrlich, *The Population Bomb*): population is the single most critical environmental factor. |
| **IPAT formula** | **I = P × A × T** (Ehrlich & Holdren). Impact = Population × Affluence (per-capita consumption / GDP) × Technology. Numbers alone don't determine impact. |
| **Barry Commoner (critic)** | **Technology** is the largest factor (petrochemical economy, pesticides, fossil fuels), far outweighing sheer numbers. |
| **Environmental Kuznets Curve** | Impact rises early in development, then falls after an affluence threshold (inverted-U). |
| **Forest Transition Theory** | Forests are cleared during development, then recover after economic transition + out-migration. |
| **Carrying Capacity** | Theoretical maximum population a system can sustain long-term. (If everyone lived like Americans, Earth could support ~2 billion.) |
| **Ecological Footprint** | Land/water area needed to sustain an individual or group; a measure of impact. |
| **Zero Population Growth (ZPG)** | Births = deaths, no net growth. |

**The opposite view — population as a source of innovation (important counterpoint):**
- **Boserup, Induced Intensification:** population growth → more food demand → forces agricultural innovation → more food on the same land. "More people = more food."
- **Green Revolution (1950s–80s):** high-yield varieties + fertilizer + pesticides + irrigation → yields soared (Indian wheat tripled 1965–80). Costs: fertilizer/pesticide dependence on oil, biodiversity loss, rising ecological cost per calorie ("no free lunch").
- **"Cornucopian"** — a pejorative critics use for the "more people is better" position.

**Is population an *effect* rather than a *cause*?**
- **Demographic Transition Model (DTM):** traditional society = high birth/high death → death rate falls first (population booms) → birth rate then falls → low birth/low death (stabilizes).
- **Women's rights are key:** India's **Kerala** state cut fertility sharply *despite* low GDP — via women's education/literacy + rural healthcare. Female education correlates strongly with low fertility. **Hartmann's thesis:** the population "problem" is not about numbers but about a **lack of rights**; the solution is to *expand* rights, not restrict them.
- **The violence and injustice of population control:** India's Emergency (1970s, Indira Gandhi) — mass sterilization camps, rationing by family size — fell hardest on the weakest, and did *not* slow growth. Population-centered thinking shifts blame onto the poor and onto women's bodies, diverting attention from the real drivers (economy, consumption). (The U.S. has ~1/4 of India's population but >5× the carbon emissions.)

> **Box — China's One-Child Policy:** from 1979, via fines + incentives, fertility fell from 2.9 to ~1.6. Seen by some as a model, by others as unnecessary and regressive.

## Lens 2 — Markets / Economics

**Core claim:** Scarcity is not an endpoint but the *engine* of market interaction; markets solve environmental problems more efficiently than regulation.

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **The Bet (Simon–Ehrlich, 1980)** | Economist Julian Simon vs. environmentalist Paul Ehrlich bet on whether 5 metals would rise/fall over 10 years. **Simon won** — prices all fell (new sources + substitutes appeared). Market optimism: more people = more good ideas. |
| **Market Response Model** | Scarcity → price rises → ① producers find new sources/tech/substitutes (supply up); ② consumers cut use / recycle / improve efficiency (demand down). E.g., higher copper price → old mines reopen. |
| **Externality** | Cost/benefit of one party's activity spills onto others (e.g., factory pollution borne by neighbors). A common root of environmental problems. |
| **Coase Theorem** | If property rights are clear *and* transaction costs are zero, externalities are solved most efficiently by **bargaining/contracting** among parties — and the outcome is optimal regardless of who initially holds the right. |
| **Coase's two preconditions** | ① rights must be exclusive and clear; ② negotiation/contracting/enforcement must be costless (zero transaction cost). **In reality neither holds** — especially for "fugitive" resources like air and global climate. This is the source of market failure. |
| **Market Failure** | When those assumptions break, markets cannot allocate efficiently. |

**Market-based solutions (be ready to give examples):**
- **Green taxes** (Pigouvian): tax pollution to "internalize" the external cost.
- **Cap and Trade:** government sets a total emissions ceiling; firms hold tradable allowances. Low-cost reducers (e.g., Plant B at $25/ton) cut more and sell allowances to high-cost reducers (Plant A at $50/ton) → same total reduction, lower total cost, more "efficient." Case: **U.S. SO₂ "acid rain" trading (from 1995).**
- **Wetland banking:** set a wetland ceiling ("no net loss"); new off-site wetlands offset local destruction.
- **Green consumption:** "vote with your dollars." Problem: **greenwashing** (exaggerated/false eco-marketing). **Green certification** (third-party) verifies claims, but certifications proliferate with inconsistent standards.

**Critiques of marketization (important):**
- **Non-market values:** a species' evolutionary, aesthetic, and moral value cannot be priced.
- **Money disconnected from nature:** markets swing wildly (bubbles/crashes), mismatched with slow ecological cycles and social values (David Harvey's critique of price as "tautology").
- **Anti-ecological discreteness:** markets trade only discrete goods/services, severing the interconnections of ecosystems.
- **Equity/democracy crisis:** handing environmental decisions to the market = handing them to "those who can pay." Wealth is extremely unequal (richest 1/5 of Americans hold 49% of income; richest 1/5 globally hold 83%), so market environmentalism is **undemocratic** (Sharon Beder: the "neutral" face of economic tools hides their political nature).

## Lens 3 — Institutions & "The Commons"

**Core claim:** Environmental problems are mostly "collective-action dilemmas," solvable by designing **rules and norms (institutions)** — without necessarily privatizing or using coercion.

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Collective-action dilemma / Free-rider** | Cooperation yields the best outcome, but each actor is tempted to "free-ride" (let others pay while they don't), causing collective failure. E.g., carbon cuts: costs are national, benefits global. |
| **Prisoner's Dilemma** | Game-theory parable: two prisoners, each fearing the other will defect (and make them the "sucker"), both confess — the worst outcome for both, even though cooperating (both silent) would have been better. |
| **Game Theory** | Established by von Neumann after WWII; first used for Cold War nuclear-deterrence analysis. **Nash Equilibrium** (John Nash, *A Beautiful Mind*) = the stable but worst outcome. |
| **Tragedy of the Commons** | **Garrett Hardin (1968, *Science*).** "A pasture open to all": each herder is incentivized to add livestock (cost shared, benefit private) → the pasture is destroyed. "Freedom in a commons brings ruin to all." |
| **Hardin's solution** | Only two roads: ① **coercion** ("mutually agreed-upon mutual coercion," which Hardin rejects over "who guards the guardians?"); ② **privatization/enclosure** (his preference, even if unjust). Both convert "open access" into "non-commons." (Note the logic overlaps with the market lens — internalizing externalities.) |

**Key critique of Hardin + the rise of the institutional school (Ostrom — very important):**
- Hardin confused **Open Access** with **Common Property.**
- **Elinor Ostrom's evidence:** many "hard-to-enclose" resources are managed successfully by **neither** coercion **nor** private property, but by **local self-governing institutions** — Maine lobster fisheries (fishers self-limit boats/traps), South Indian irrigation systems, East African tree-use traditions.
- **Institutions** = rules and norms (formal laws + informal norms) that constrain individual behavior.

**Ostrom's design principles for sustainable commons (high-frequency — memorize):**
1. **Boundaries** — clearly define who may use and the resource's extent.
2. **Proportionality** — costs match benefits.
3. **Collective choice** — those affected help make the rules.
4. **Monitoring** — track user behavior and resource condition.
5. **Sanctions** — *graduated* penalties for violators; encourage voluntary compliance, with coercion a last resort.
6. **Conflict resolution** — low-cost dispute mechanisms.
7. **Autonomy** — external authorities recognize the group's right to self-organize (minimal non-interference).

**Critiques of the institutional school:**
- Game theory assumes parties have *equal power*; in reality power is highly unequal (gender, caste). E.g., Indian village *panchayats* (decision bodies) dominated by elite men, while women who do the water-fetching/irrigation labor hold no property rights → rules lack legitimacy.
- **Scale problem:** can the success of small local commons "scale up" to the global commons (e.g., climate)? This may decide the planet's future.

> **Box — Montreal Protocol:** widely seen as a *successful* environmental institution (controlling CFCs / protecting the ozone layer).

## Lens 4 — Ethics

**Core claim:** Rethink humanity's place among all things. (No separate assigned reading, but runs through the tuna/uranium chapters.)
- Key tension: **anthropocentric** vs. **ecocentric / animal-rights** views.
- Tuna-chapter ethics: dolphin "rights" vs. species protection — "Can a victory of *rights* produce an ecological *defeat*?" (see Module 5).

## Lens 5 — Risk & Hazards

**Core claim:** The environment is inherently uncertain; use **risk assessment** to make the "most rational" decisions. (Yucca Mountain is the best example.)

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Risk Assessment** | Rigorous use of logic and information to determine the risk (probability of an adverse outcome) of a decision, to reach a more rational result. |
| **Risk Communication** | How best to present risk information to the public. |
| **Core critique (Shrader-Frechette)** | Long-term geological risk assessment relies on *speculative, scientifically unverifiable* geological models. "Geology is an interpretive, not a predictive, science." |
| **Maxey Flats case (KY, 1962)** | Low-level waste site; the model predicted plutonium would migrate half an inch in 24,000 years; **in reality it moved 2 miles in 10 years** — a total failure of siting risk assessment. |

> Related: **Jevons Paradox** — efficiency gains lower price and may *increase* total resource use. **Risk-perception bias** — the public fears low-probability hazards (e.g., nuclear) and underestimates high-impact ones (e.g., poverty).

## Lens 6 — Political Economy

**Core claim:** The root of the environmental crisis is the **economy**, and the economy is rooted in **power relations** — who gets what, who works for whom, who bears the costs. Opposite to the market lens: it stresses how the market economy *itself* corrodes the environment.

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Core thesis** | Nature–society relations can't be understood apart from the economy; the economy is never separable from politics (hence "political economy"). The fundamental driver is **capital accumulation.** |
| **Environmental Justice** | Environmental "goods" (parks, clean air) and "bads" (pollution, hazards, waste) should be distributed fairly across all people regardless of race, ethnicity, gender. **Environmental injustice** = dangerous/unhealthy environments disproportionately sited next to minority/marginalized communities. |
| **"Green consumption is still consumption"** | From a political-economy view, the *voluntariness* of market solutions is their fatal flaw; it lets a privileged few keep consuming and never reaches the root. |
| **Second Contradiction of Capitalism** | Production destroys the very conditions it depends on (e.g., the tuna industry slaughters dolphins and wrecks the ecosystem it relies on). |
| **Uneven Development** | Capitalism's tendency to produce starkly unequal wealth/poverty and production/consumption across places; the affluent (who emit most) are least affected, and the worst-affected lack money to "express preferences" in markets. |

**Classic cases (be ready to discuss):**
- **Navajo uranium mining** (see Module 3): colonial division of labor; miner lung-cancer rates many times the national average; white Grand Junction got $250M cleanup while the Navajo were ignored.
- **Global e-waste flows** (see Module 3): low-GDP countries become net importers; Agbogbloshie (Ghana), Guiyu (China).

## Lens 7 — Social Construction of Nature

**Core claim:** People understand and interpret the environment through **language, stories, images (discourse)**; these narratives are often inherited or imposed by media/government/education/industry, and are *not* harmless — they enable or conceal real actions and consequences.

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Discourse** | Not merely a "representation" of the world but statements and texts embedded in power that (partly) *construct* the world we inhabit. |
| **Terra Nullius ("nobody's land")** | A legal construct of Australian colonization: claiming land was "owned by no one" and thus open to seizure. But Indigenous peoples already lived there — they simply lacked the modern construct of "private property." |
| **"Wilderness" vs. "development" discourse** | See the Australian Ranger mine / Kakadu case: what counts as "appropriate" land use is shaped by discourse. |

> **🎯 Lens self-test:** Can you state each lens's *different* answer to "What is the root of global warming, and how do we solve it?"
> - **Population:** too many people → control population / empower women
> - **Markets:** externality unpriced → carbon tax / trading / green consumption
> - **Institutions:** climate is a global-commons free-rider problem → international institutions (Kyoto, etc.)
> - **Risk:** the future is uncertain → risk assessment to guide mitigation investment
> - **Political Economy:** capital accumulation drives it → radical structural change, distrust "green consumption"
> - **Social Construction:** how we "tell the story" of climate shapes action

## MODULE 2 — Energy: Global & Local Impacts

> Natural science: R&F C5 (Energy), C8 (Global Change), C7 (Air Pollution). Social science: RHM C9 (Carbon Dioxide), C6 (Risk & Hazards).

### 2.1 Energy basics (R&F C5)
- **The energy gap:** U.S. per-capita energy use is ~**25×** India's — this energy does work that Indians do by human muscle.
- **Temperature vs. heat:** temperature measures the rate of atomic vibration (a property of an object); heat is a *quantity* (like grams or gallons). `heat ∝ mass × temperature change`. Conversion: `°C = 5/9(°F − 32)`.
- **Energy categories:** direct (solar, wind, hydro) vs. stored (fossil fuels, batteries); renewable vs. non-renewable (fossil fuels and uranium are non-renewable).
- **Fossil fuels:** formed by decay of organic matter under **anoxic** conditions; products are **hydrocarbons** (only H and C). Coal (carbonized wood), oil (transformed marine fossils), **natural gas = methane CH₄** (lightest, simplest). **Combustion:** hydrocarbon + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O + energy.
- **Nuclear** (see Module 3): radioactive decay, **fission**, fusion. Nuclear ≈ **16%** of global electricity; coal ≈ **40%.**
- **Energy storage:** batteries; **hydrogen H₂** (made by electrolysis, burns like natural gas); **pumped storage** (pump water uphill off-peak, release at peak — net energy is *lost*; used only for peak-shaving).
> **Boxes:** China's Three Gorges Dam (hydro); Exxon Valdez oil spill (1989); Chernobyl (1986).

### 2.2 The carbon cycle & CO₂ (R&F C8 + RHM C9) ★ debate core
- **Carbon distribution:** ~**99% of Earth's carbon is locked in crustal sedimentary rock**; the rest cycles among oceans (the largest reservoir), atmosphere, plants, soil.
- **Carbon cycle:**
  - **Photosynthesis:** plants use solar energy to convert CO₂ + H₂O → sugars (tissue), releasing O₂. The basis of all life on Earth.
  - **Carbon sequestration:** capturing carbon from the atmosphere and storing it in the biosphere or geosphere (plant photosynthesis, or engineered methods).
  - Humans dig up and burn coal/oil, releasing carbon to the atmosphere **far faster than it naturally returns to geological storage.**
- **History of atmospheric CO₂:**
  - Early Earth (~2 Gya): high CO₂, almost no O₂; cyanobacteria photosynthesis produced O₂ → atmosphere shifted from high-CO₂ to oxygenated ("Great Oxidation"). Ancient anaerobes were "poisoned" by the oxygen they themselves produced.
  - **Lesson:** life can change the planet's biochemistry — often to the point that it must re-adapt to survive.
  - Today: ~78% N₂, 21% O₂, CO₂ < 0.1%. **⚠️ Data update:** the text says ~381 ppm, but the latest measurement gives **428 ppm (Jan 2026)** (yearly: 2017 ≈ 406 → 2020 ≈ 413 → 2023 ≈ 419 → 2026 ≈ 428), still accelerating.
- **Keeling Curve:** Charles Keeling measured atmospheric CO₂ at **Mauna Loa**, Hawaii, from **1958**. The curve rises steeply overall with annual seasonal wobble (Northern Hemisphere plants take up CO₂ in spring → dip; release in autumn → rise). Hawaii was chosen for having no local industry, representing the global atmosphere.
  - **Wallace Broecker (1931–2019, "the prophet of climate change")** — among the first to predict anthropogenic CO₂ would cause global warming; **introduced "global warming" into the scientific literature in the 1970s**; his ocean-chemistry work mapped global ocean circulation and its powerful influence on climate.
- **"Carbon civilization":** modern civilization is fundamentally a carbon civilization — building, transport, food production (fertilizers/pesticides themselves derive from oil) all burn fossil fuels.
- **The double blow of deforestation:** ① destroys forests that sequester carbon (weakening capture); ② burning releases the carbon stored in the trees.

#### Greenhouse effect & climate science (R&F C8)
- **Functions of the atmosphere:** provides O₂, provides CO₂ for photosynthesis, provides rain, **absorbs solar energy re-radiated from the surface** (without this, mean surface temperature would be ~30°C lower and water would freeze), and ozone absorbs UV.
- **Greenhouse gases (GHGs)** and their share of thermal-IR absorption (text data): **H₂O ≈ 65%, CO₂ ≈ 32%, CH₄ ≈ 1%**, with N₂O/O₃/CFCs trace.
- **Four sources of methane CH₄** (each ~500 Mt/yr; atmospheric lifetime ~10 yr): ① ruminant digestion (cattle, sheep); ② anaerobic decay in natural wetlands/tundra; ③ rice paddies; ④ fossil-fuel combustion. ② is natural; livestock/paddies/fossil fuels are anthropogenic. Pre-industrial 0.7 ppm → now ~1.7 ppm.
- **Three controls on climate:** ① surface + Earth's rotation (latitude → wind belts: 0–30° trades/easterlies, 30–60° westerlies); ② atmospheric composition; ③ the ocean "conveyor belt" (thermohaline circulation distributes heat).
- **Ice-age cycles:** ~every **100,000 years** (slow cooling 80–90 kyr + rapid warming 15–20 kyr), driven mainly by **changes in Earth's orbit (Milankovitch cycles).**
- **Evidence & attribution of anthropogenic warming:** global mean temperature rose ~**0.5°C** over the past century (text data), ~**20×** the natural warming rate since the last ice age. Solar activity is ruled out (not unusually high over the past century); main cause is rising CO₂/CH₄/CFCs.
- **IPCC projection:** global mean temperature will rise **2–6°C** by 2100.
- **Six feedback/complexity mechanisms** (may amplify or dampen): ① oceans absorb more CO₂ (dampens); ② more clouds reflect sunlight (dampens; industrial aerosols similar); ③ CO₂ boosts plant growth/carbon fixation (dampens); ④ warming oceans release CO₂ (amplifies); ⑤ polar ice/snow melt lowers albedo (amplifies); ⑥ thermohaline conveyor shutdown (could warm >5°C within a century).

#### Week 3 supplement: RHM Ch.9 "six puzzles" + latest IPCC (debate essentials)
- **★ RHM Ch.9 "The Puzzle" (the organizing spine of the chapter and the debate):**
  1. Atmospheric CO₂ has changed over millennia; **the conditions for life on Earth can be altered by living things and by humans** (echoes the Great Oxidation).
  2. The rise of industrial production = the rise of an economy and society **completely dependent on carbon.**
  3. CO₂ (with other GHGs) drives **severe but unpredictable** global climate change.
  4. **Institutions view:** achieve international cooperation through prudent compromise and effective rule design.
  5. **Markets view:** stress the efficiency of green consumption and "tradable atmospheric rights."
  6. **Political-economy view:** economical decarbonization schemes have inherent limits and are undemocratic because the problem is rooted in the economy itself.
  → Puzzles 1–3 are science; puzzles 4–6 are the three-lens analysis (§2.4).
- **Greenhouse effect (definition):** the process by which atmospheric gases trap **long-wave radiation (heat)**; it is **natural and necessary** for life — the problem is the *extra* anthropogenic GHGs (CO₂, H₂O(g), CH₄, N₂O).
- **★ IPCC AR5 (5th Assessment, 2013–14) key conclusions:**
  - Human influence on the climate system is **clear**; "**extremely likely** that human influence was the dominant cause of warming since the mid-20th century."
  - Each of the past decades since 1850 has been warmer than the preceding one.
  - **>90% of the energy accumulated in the climate system 1971–2010 went into the oceans** (so oceans keep warming).
  - **2010 GHG emissions by source (high-frequency):** energy sector 35%, agriculture/forestry/land use 24%, industry 21%, transport 14%, buildings 6.4% — energy production is the leading driver.
  - **Carbon Budget:** the total compatible with a 2°C target is ~**2900 Gt CO₂**; ~**1900 Gt (≈65%)** was used 1870–2011, leaving ~1000 Gt → "the window for action is closing fast."
  - **Cost of the 2°C target:** global emissions must fall **40–70%** by 2050 and near-zero by 2100; estimated to reduce economic growth by only ~**0.06%** ("delaying growth, not abandoning it"), excluding the benefits of avoided climate damage.
  - **Mitigation:** ① higher efficiency; ② more low-/zero-carbon energy (most tech already exists); ③ better carbon sinks (less deforestation, afforestation, BECCS); ④ lifestyle and behavior change.
- **★ IPCC AR6 (6th Assessment) headline conclusions (stronger):**
  - Human activity has **unequivocally** caused global warming; **global surface temperature 2011–2020 was 1.1°C above 1850–1900.**
  - **Environmental-justice dimension:** vulnerable communities that contributed least are disproportionately affected.
  - Current national pledges (NDCs as of Oct 2021) make it **likely** warming exceeds 1.5°C this century.
  - Limiting warming requires **net-zero CO₂**; emissions from existing fossil infrastructure alone would exceed the 1.5°C remaining budget.
  - "The choices and actions of this decade will have impacts for thousands of years."
- **"Global Boiling":** July 2023, UN Secretary-General Guterres: "The era of global warming has ended; the era of global boiling has arrived."

### 2.3 Air pollution & the Air Quality Index (AQI) (R&F C7 + urban-air lecture / Aubrecht Ch14–15) ★ indoor-air lab background + standalone exam topic

**A. Two eras & acid rain (R&F C7 macro background)**
- **Two eras:** ① the coal era (pre-~1950): coal burning emitted ash, tar, SO₂, NOx → London "pea-soup" fogs, blackened buildings; ② the automobile era (mid–late 20th c.): exhaust, lead (industrial nations banned leaded gasoline ~1980), organics → smog.
- **Acid rain:**
  - Normal rain is weakly acidic (pH ~5.6–6.5) because dissolved CO₂ forms carbonic acid.
  - Industrial **SO₂ → sulfuric acid H₂SO₄** and **NOx → nitric acid HNO₃** (strong, fully ionizing) push rain pH below 5. Industrial sulfur emissions are now ~**2×** all natural processes.
  - Harms: lake acidification → fish kills ("dead lakes"); soil nutrient leaching, toxic aluminum (Al) released; forest decline (yellowing pines).
  - Worst areas: eastern U.S./eastern Canada, Europe (downwind of westerly-belt industry). The **"Black Triangle"** = the high-sulfur-coal region at the Germany/Czech/Poland (Silesia) border.
- **Smog** (smoke + fog): incomplete gasoline combustion → unburned gasoline + CO + NOx + **ozone O₃** (photochemical smog; mechanism in D). R&F line: "**20% of the world has 80% of the cars.**" In poor countries, coal-soot SO₂ mixes with vehicle exhaust to become *more* toxic (coal era + auto era stacked).

**B. ★ Air Quality Index (AQI) — lecture core**
- **Definition/use:** converts concentrations of multiple pollutants into a single, comparable, public-friendly index for daily air-quality reporting and health guidance. (*What is AQI used for?* → to communicate pollution levels and corresponding health risks/precautions on a uniform scale.)
- **AQI is based on 5 "criteria pollutants"** (regulated by the Clean Air Act): ① ground-level ozone (O₃); ② particulate matter (PM2.5/PM10); ③ carbon monoxide (CO); ④ sulfur dioxide (SO₂); ⑤ nitrogen dioxide (NO₂).
- **NAAQS (National Ambient Air Quality Standards):** EPA sets a health-protective concentration limit for each pollutant. **Calculation rule (high-frequency): compute each pollutant's AQI separately, then take the MAXIMUM as the overall AQI** — "the worst pollutant sets today's AQI."

  | AQI range | Category | Color |
  |---|---|---|
  | 0–50 | Good | Green |
  | 51–100 | Moderate | Yellow |
  | 101–150 | Unhealthy for Sensitive Groups | Orange |
  | 151–200 | Unhealthy | Red |
  | 201–300 | Very Unhealthy | Purple |
  | 301–500 | Hazardous | Maroon |

- **★ U.S.–China AQI differences:** both are called "AQI," but the **breakpoints/standards differ**, so the *same* PM2.5 concentration yields *different* AQI numbers in the two countries — don't compare numbers naively. Two observed trends: ① Chinese cities (e.g., Beijing) improved markedly over the past decade; ② the western U.S. now suffers AQI spikes from **wildfires** (Seattle once "worse than Beijing") — pollution is no longer an "Asia-only" issue.

**C. ★ Atmospheric inversion (commonly tested)**
- Normally temperature falls with altitude; warm air below, cool above → vertical convection disperses pollutants upward.
- **Inversion layer:** an abnormal "warm-over-cool" layer acts like a "lid," **suppressing vertical convection and trapping surface emissions near the ground** → AQI spikes.
- Role: inversion doesn't *produce* pollution but **sets the conditions** for it to be trapped; basin/valley terrain (LA, Beijing) + inversion produces severe events. (*What role does inversion play?* → it traps surface emissions near the ground, preventing dispersal and worsening pollution.)

**D. ★ Ozone's "two faces" & the photochemical-smog mechanism (describe in 3–4 sentences)**
- **Stratospheric ozone ("good ozone," high up):** formed ~40 km up by UV; absorbs UV and protects surface life. (O₂ + UV → 2 O; 2 O + 2 O₂ + M → 2 O₃ + M.)
- **Tropospheric/photochemical ozone ("bad ozone," at the surface):** an urban pollutant irritating the airways. Without anthropogenic emissions, surface background O₃ ≈ 0.025–0.045 ppm.
- **★ Core mechanism (be able to explain why exhaust makes O₃ *accumulate*):**
  1. NO₂ photolyzes: **NO₂ + hν → NO + O.**
  2. Atomic O makes ozone: **O + O₂ + M → O₃ + M.**
  3. **Ozone titration:** the O₃ is consumed by NO: **O₃ + NO → NO₂ + O₂** — normally this "eats" the O₃ and prevents accumulation.
  4. **The key role of VOCs:** when exhaust brings large amounts of **VOCs** (reactive organic gases), VOCs (via peroxy radicals) oxidize NO directly to NO₂: **VOC + NO → NO₂.** This path does *not* consume O₃ but *uses up the NO* → the step-3 titration slows for lack of NO → **O₃ accumulates (>0.050 ppm)**, forming photochemical smog.
  - One-line version: **NOx + VOC + sunlight → ozone + other oxidants.** Precursors are NOx and VOCs; VOCs lift the titration "brake" on ozone.
- **Diurnal pattern:** morning rush → NO rises → NO converts to NO₂ by late morning → O₃ peaks at midday (strongest sun). **LA smog** is the classic case (brownish/orange haze, strong ozone smell).

**E. Transport sources (Aubrecht Ch15.3):** vehicles are the main source of **CO, VOCs, NOx**; not the main source of PM10 or SO₂, but the most dangerous fine particles, **PM2.5, come almost entirely from engine and stove combustion** — pointing both "AQI killers" (ozone + fine PM) at vehicles.

**F. Health & cost:** particulates differ in **size and composition**; smaller → deeper penetration — coarse particles are caught in the upper airways, but **PM2.5 reaches the bronchioles and alveoli** (gas-exchange sites) and enters the blood, doing the most harm. Historical disaster: **1952 London Great Smog** (coal soot + inversion, thousands dead). Air pollution costs both **health and money** (improving AQI saves both).

**G. ★ Key legislation: U.S. Clean Air Act (1970, amended 1990)** — *Which law improved U.S. air quality?* → the **Clean Air Act.** It established NAAQS and the 5 criteria-pollutant limits; the 1990 amendment introduced acid-rain SO₂ cap-and-trade (see §2.4).

> **★ Indoor-air-quality lab links:** ① indoor combustion (cooking, heating, smoking) produces CO, particulates, NOx — the same pollutants AQI tracks; ② ventilation is key — essentially avoiding an "indoor inversion" where pollutants accumulate; ③ measured PM/CO can be compared to NAAQS/AQI to judge health risk.

### 2.4 The institutions / markets / political-economy analysis of CO₂ (RHM C9) ★ debate essentials

Analyze carbon through three lenses (this is the debate training; corresponds to puzzles 4–6):

**① Institutions lens: climate free-riders & carbon cooperation**
- Carbon is the **largest global-commons** prisoner's dilemma/free-rider problem: impacts don't necessarily occur where emitted; all benefit from cuts, but **only those who actually cut pay the cost** → hard to cooperate voluntarily. Core question: **who pays?**
- **Kyoto Protocol (memorize the timeline):** UNFCCC entered force **1994** → 1995 negotiations launched → text adopted **1997** → entered force **16 Feb 2005.** Set **binding reduction targets** for major economies, ranging −8% to +10% (vs. each country's **1990** level); commitment period **2008–2012**, ≥**5%** below 1990 overall. Same difficulties as the fisheries commons: hard to monitor, hard to sanction free-riders (the U.S. never ratified).
- **★ Paris Agreement:** adopted **12 Dec 2015** by **196 parties**, a **legally binding** international coordination framework. The core mechanism shifted to nationally-determined **NDCs** — but IPCC AR6 notes current NDCs are insufficient to hold 1.5°C.
- **Developed vs. developing equity dispute (debate-frequent):** developed countries became "developed" by emitting most of the atmospheric CO₂ and have **higher per-capita** emissions; developing countries are low per-capita but grow fast in population. Because CO₂ persists in the atmosphere **at least a century**, the U.S.'s historical responsibility is **>3×** China's. → Designing emission incentives fairly is the central institutional puzzle.
- **Post-Kyoto:** REDD (Reducing Emissions from Deforestation) — emitters in rich countries pay (often poor/Indigenous) communities to maintain forest carbon sinks.

**② Markets lens: trade the gas, buy the reduction**
- Market-environmentalist view: emissions are an **externality**; command-and-control regulation is inefficient; **market solutions** best internalize the cost. Two main routes: carbon tax / cap-and-trade; plus carbon offsets and green carbon consumption.
- **Green carbon consumption / carbon offsetting:** consumers pay producers not to emit (buy low-emission products) or pay to offset their own carbon (e.g., tree-planting; Carbonfund example: a Chicago–London flight seat = 3.82 t carbon, offset for $38.53). RHM includes a "strange logic of carbon offsetting" cartoon mocking its absurdity.
- **Carbon markets / cap-and-trade:** set an emissions cap; would-be over-emitters **buy emission rights** from those who can cut cheaply. Cases: European Climate Exchange ECX (opened 2005); **★ Shenzhen ETS (local case)** — launched **18 Jun 2013**, cap 34.78 MtCO₂e, covering ~**40%** of Shenzhen's emissions, 635 firms + 197 public buildings; industry **95% free allocation**, 3% auction, 2% reserve; buildings 100% free.
- **★★ Carbon Tax vs. Cap-and-Trade — the core debate showdown (argue both sides):**
  - Carbon-tax advocates (Carbon Tax Center, Komanoff & Rosenblum): tax coal/oil/gas by **carbon content per BTU** → cut use, drive clean substitutes and efficiency, raise revenue. "No more free dumping" — the atmosphere has been a free carbon dump since the Industrial Revolution; making emitters pay forces clean tech. "Starter" tax: $37/ton carbon = 10¢/gal gasoline = 0.72¢/kWh → cuts U.S. CO₂ ~4%, repeat 10×.
  - **Key design — tax-shifting (not a tax hike):** use carbon revenue to **cut regressive payroll/sales taxes** and protect low-income households; "tax pollution, not productive labor." Co-benefits: efficiency, R&D, less dependence on foreign oil (national security), money stays domestic.

  | Dimension | Carbon Tax | Cap-and-Trade |
  |---|---|---|
  | Price predictability | **Predictable** price, good for long-term low-carbon investment | **Volatile** price, deters useful investment |
  | Speed to implement | **Fast**, quick effect | **Complex, contested, slow** (cap/allocation/certification/offsets/penalties...) |
  | Equity | Can be **revenue-neutral** | Often allocates by **historical emissions** → "windfall" to polluters, may even **reward emitting more now** to inflate the baseline |
  | Transparency | **Direct, transparent, simple** price signal | **Complex, opaque,** limits public participation |
  | Coverage | **All sectors** | Mostly just electricity (~**40%** of emissions) |
  - Note: the carbon-tax camp also criticizes "efficiency standards/subsidies alone" — efficiency standards are too slow and gamed (CAFE loopholes spawned SUVs), passive, piecemeal; clean-energy subsidies often become lobbyist/special-interest spoils, and many "new energies" still emit.

**③ Political-economy lens: who killed the atmosphere?**
- "Green consumption is still consumption" — **voluntariness is the fatal weakness** of market solutions. The benefits of carbon emission accrue to a tiny minority who have no interest in being institutionally constrained, and push more consumption as the way out.
- **Uneven development:** the richest emit most yet are least affected; the worst-affected have **no money to "express preferences"** in markets.
- **Second contradiction of capital:** accumulation requires consumption → consumption depletes resources, produces waste, degrades the environment.
- **Critique of carbon markets:** greenwashing; no guarantee offset projects actually offset carbon; offset projects may benefit rich countries while harming poor ones (e.g., Lohmann's Uganda case: a Norwegian coal-burning firm bought 20,000 ha but planted 600 ha, evicted hundreds of families, created 43 jobs, sequestered far less carbon than claimed).
- **The soul-question + conclusion:** "If capital accumulation got us into this mess, why trust it to get us out?" Political economists conclude: **markets and institutions have not actually reduced atmospheric carbon — but have successfully concentrated power and wealth.** They therefore prefer **regulatory** solutions, because the root is the economy itself.

### 2.5 Coal's pollution: acid deposition · global dimming · mercury in fish (Week 2-2 / Aubrecht Ch14)

> Lecture hooks: "Is there such a thing as 'clean coal'?" and "What links coal and sushi?" (answer: mercury). Logic: coal → 4 impacts: sulfur-smog / acid rain / global dimming (all from coal's S) + mercury in fish (from coal's Hg).

**A. Coal consumption, geology, composition**
- **Coal supplies ~half of U.S. electricity.**
- Key composition (for estimating emissions): **sulfur S ≈ 2% by weight**, **mercury Hg ≈ 0.1 mg/kg**; U.S. consumes ~**1200 million short tons/yr** (1 short ton = 907 kg).
- **Regional differences (test point):** U.S. eastern/interior coal vs. western coal differ markedly in S and Hg (eastern is higher-sulfur) → emissions and controls vary by region.
- **★ Emission estimate (typical calculation):** S emission ≈ 2% × 1200 Mt = **24 million short tons**; Hg emission ≈ 0.1 mg/kg × 1200 Mt × 1000 kg/t ≈ **120 tons.**
- **Combustion reactions:** C + O₂ → CO₂; S + O₂ → SO₂ (→ H₂SO₄); HgS + O₂ → SO₂ + Hg. Atmospheric mercury exists as elemental, gaseous, and particle-adsorbed forms.

**B. Acid deposition (finer than §2.3)**
- Split into **wet deposition** (acid rain/snow) and **dry deposition** (acidic particles/gases). Monitored in the U.S. by **NAPAP.**
- **Two acids (Name two acids):** sulfuric **H₂SO₄**, nitric **HNO₃.**
- **★ pH–hydrogen-ion conversion (test calculation):** each 1-unit pH drop = **10×** H⁺. E.g., Lake A pH=7, Lake B pH=4 → **B has 1000× (10³)** more H⁺ than A.
- **Harms (5):** respiratory disease; soil nutrient leaching → forest damage; lake acidification → fishery loss; toxic metals (Al) leached → drinking-water contamination; corrosion of buildings (limestone).
- **Aquatic data:** at **pH < 4.5, aluminum is released and lakes are fishless.** "Fishless lakes": Norway+Sweden 16,000, Canada 14,000; "threatened lakes": Norway+Sweden 52,000, Canada 150,000, U.S. 9,000.
- **Evidence that coal plants are the main cause of eastern-U.S. acid precipitation:** concentrated high-sulfur-coal burning in the east + prevailing westerlies carry pollutants to the northeast/Canada; downwind acid deposition spatially matches the sources.
- **"Clean coal":** EPA defines it as (not-yet-widespread) technology that significantly cuts SO₂ or NOx before/during/after combustion. The lecture is skeptical ("politicians can be very confusing — and confused"), echoing "is there really clean coal?"

**C. Global dimming**
- **Definition:** a measurable decline in sunlight reaching the surface, ~late 1950s–early 1990s (~**5% globally**, ~10% in the U.S., **37% in Hong Kong**).
- **Two causes:** ① pollution particles reflect incoming sunlight back to space; ② pollution makes air condense more water droplets → darker, thicker clouds blocking more light.
- **Significance:** coal-burning aerosols have a **net cooling effect** — this masks GHG warming, complicating the climate problem (cleaning up haze may "unmask" hidden warming).

**D. Mercury in fish ("coal & sushi," links to Module 5)**
- **Methylation:** inorganic mercury from coal → converted in the environment to organic **methylmercury** (readily bioabsorbed).
- **★ Bioaccumulation / biomagnification (core):** methylmercury concentrates up the food chain and resists excretion — **bigger/older fish and higher trophic levels carry more mercury** (explains why big fish are higher in mercury; echoes the tuna chapter). USGS: in 291 sampled streams, **every fish** had detectable methylmercury; ~1/4 exceeded the EPA safe limit.
- **Why pregnant women should be cautious:** methylmercury can cause **fetal neurodevelopmental damage** (prenatal exposure is most harmful). EPA guidance: whole-blood Hg < **5 µg/L (ppb)**, hair Hg < **1 µg/g (ppm).**
- **Trend & China:** U.S. fish "advisories" rose from 1,233 (1993) to 3,089 (2003). **China's** anthropogenic Hg emission was 536 t in 1999, ~**38%** from coal; China is the world's largest coal producer/consumer (Chinese coal Hg ≈ 0.16 mg/kg).

### 3.1 Mineral-resource basics (R&F C6)
- Proverb: "If you can't grow it or catch it, you have to mine it." Almost every product depends on minerals.
- **Eight ages of humanity** (marked by mineral use): Paleolithic → Neolithic → Copper → Bronze → Iron → Coal → Oil → Nuclear.
- **Two definitions of "mineral" (distinguish):**
  - *Geological:* a naturally occurring, inorganic, crystalline solid of limited compositional range. ("A mineral is to a rock as a cell is to an organ"; rock = an aggregate of one or more minerals.)
  - *Economic:* a **mineral resource** = rock/mineral that ① someone will pay for, and ② exists in enough quantity to be mined, processed, transported, and delivered **profitably.**
- **★ Economics defines what is a resource:** limestone by Lake Michigan is a valuable resource (near water transport, high demand), but the same limestone in Nebraska/Ecuador is not; Spain's Almadén mercury mine thrived for a millennium, but mercury demand collapsed as environmental awareness rose, slashing its resource value.
- **★ Resource vs. Reserve (high-frequency):**
  - **Reserves** = identified + currently economically extractable portion (subdivided proven / probable / possible).
  - As exploration, price, and technology change, resources can be "upgraded" to reserves — explaining why reserves can *increase* during mining (as with copper, whose price fell with extraction).
- **Sustainable vs. non-sustainable resources:** minerals are non-renewable.
- **Properties of minerals:** ① **fixed location** (unlike farms/fisheries, a mine is where it is; you must negotiate with the property holder); ② **discovery risk** (ore is often buried under worthless "waste rock" with no surface sign).
- **Legal/property:** **Regalian (state-owned) vs. accessory (surface-owner's) mineral rights;** U.S. public-lands mining (1872 Mining Law).
- **USGS global assessment (from 2002):** the first globally consistent estimate of *undiscovered* nonfuel mineral resources, estimating "probable amounts" in major deposit types 1–3 km below the surface (representative minerals: Cu, platinum-group elements, potash).

### 3.2 Uranium — the paradox of utility and hazard (RHM C12) ★ discussion 1 core

#### Science & history
- Uranium = element **92**, the **heaviest natural element**, formed in supernovae, ~2–4 ppm in the crust.
- **Radioactivity:** unstable, emits particles/energy until decaying to a more stable lighter element (uranium ultimately decays to **lead**). Half-lives 0.7–4.5 billion years.
- **Discovery:** Klaproth (1789, named for Uranus) → Becquerel (accidental discovery of radioactivity) → the Curies (radium; 1903 Nobel; both had radiation sickness). Pierre Curie's question: "Is it right to probe so deeply into nature's secrets?"
- **Nuclear physics:** neutron (1932) → fission (1938, uranium split into barium by neutron bombardment).
- **Nuclear fission:** a nucleus splits into two lighter nuclei + releases energy + releases neutrons.
- **Chain reaction:** released neutrons strike other nuclei, cascading. Controlled → nuclear power; uncontrolled → nuclear bomb.
- **Isotopes & enrichment:** natural uranium is >99% **U-238 (non-fissile)** and <1% **U-235 (fissile)**. **Enrichment** = raising the U-235 fraction.
- **Manhattan Project** (from 1939; Oak Ridge, Los Alamos; Fermi, Oppenheimer) → Hiroshima bomb 6 Aug 1945 (~150,000 dead), Nagasaki.
- Post-war, nuclear split two ways: weapons + "peaceful" power (the **USSR's first grid-connected reactor, 1954**).
- **Nuclear fuel chain:** mining → milling → enrichment → fuel fabrication → reactor → **spent fuel / radwaste**; every step carries risk, pollution, toxic waste.

> **Box — Energy-Efficient Buildings:** reducing demand is easier than building more nuclear/coal plants. U.S. buildings use ~40% of national energy; retrofits cut use ~30% on average. Note the **Jevons Paradox**: efficiency lowers price and may increase total use.

#### Risk & Hazards lens: Yucca Mountain high-level-waste disposal (★ best risk-assessment case)
- **Spent fuel / radwaste** (high- and low-level) is dangerous for an extraordinarily long time: Strontium-90 (behaves like calcium → bone cancer/leukemia) dangerous for centuries; other decay products for tens to hundreds of thousands of years. Humans have been an industrial species only ~250 years, yet are making pollutants dangerous for >100× that.
- **Four disposal options for high-level waste:**
  1. Reactor-site cooling pools (need continuous power; temporary only);
  2. On-site **dry casks** (good safety record but costly);
  3. **Reprocessing** (reuse in other reactors, but produces **plutonium → proliferation risk**; nonproliferation advocates strongly oppose);
  4. **Deep geological disposal** (permanent burial in a stable geological repository) — no operating high-level repository exists yet (Finland building one).
- **Yucca Mountain (Nevada) siting history:**
  - 1982 Nuclear Waste Policy Act (NWPA) required a permanent deep repository; siting criteria: ① no groundwater intrusion (prevent corrosion/leakage); ② minimal seismic activity.
  - 1987 amendment designated Yucca Mountain as the **only** site for further study — nicknamed the "**Screw Nevada bill**" (politics over science).
  - EPA standard: 15 millirem/resident/year, applicable for **10,000 years.** DOE recommendation approved by Congress in 2002.
- **Critiques of the Yucca Mountain risk assessment:**
  - 2004 federal appeals court ruled the 10,000-year standard "arbitrary and capricious," ordering EPA to extend it to **1,000,000 years.**
  - **Shrader-Frechette:** long-term geological risk assessment relies on speculative, unverifiable models; "geology is an interpretive, not a predictive, science." Cites **Maxey Flats (KY, 1962):** model predicted plutonium would migrate half an inch in 24,000 years; in reality it moved 2 miles in 10 years.
  - 2010: Obama (pushed by Nevada Senator Harry Reid) shelved Yucca Mountain.
- **Core question (environmental-justice preview):** is it fair to make a state with *no* nuclear plants store the most dangerous waste of the other 43?

#### Political-economy / environmental-justice lens: Navajo uranium mining (★ memorize)
- The Cold War drove uranium demand; Navajo land held the largest U.S. uranium deposits. **Colonial division of labor:** whites as managers/engineers, Navajo as low-paid miners ($1.5–2/hr).
- **Cancer arrives:** before the 1950s the Navajo had almost no cancer; from radon and "radon daughters" exposure + long-term lack of protection (no respirators, no water-spraying for dust, no ventilation), 1980s studies found Navajo miners' lung-cancer rate was **56× the national average.**
- **Tailings-housing tragedy:** uranium-bearing tailings were used as building material. In 1968, the (almost all-white) town of Grand Junction was found to have radioactive houses; the government quickly spent **$250M** demolishing/rebuilding 4,000 homes — while the same problem on the Navajo Reservation was ignored; a 1975 EPA cleanup recommendation was rejected twice by two federal agencies (including the Indian Health Service), and the Navajo lived in radioactive homes for another 20 years.
- **Church Rock 1979 liquid-tailings spill:** to date the largest environmental release of radioactive material in U.S. history.
- 2005: the Navajo tribal government unanimously **permanently banned** uranium mining on tribal land.
- **★ RECA (Radiation Exposure Compensation Act, 1990):** from WWII to 1971, the **U.S. government was the sole buyer** of uranium ore. Despite European evidence that uranium mining causes lung cancer, **protection was almost nonexistent before 1962** and slow/incomplete after; high miner illness eventually drove RECA. (Brugge & Goble, 2002.)
- **★ Carcinogenic mechanism:** Bale & Harley (1951) showed it is **not radon gas itself but the "radon daughters/progeny"** (Po-218, Pb-214, Bi-214, Po-214) that lodge near sensitive lung cells and irradiate them by their half-lives — a large internal dose. (2000 Navajo study: of 94 lung-cancer deaths 1969–93, 63 were former uranium miners; **miner relative risk 28.6**.)

#### Social-construction lens: Australia's Ranger mine (discourse)
- Australia has *no* nuclear plants yet exports nearly 100% of the uranium it mines; the Ranger mine (world's 2nd-largest uranium producer) is surrounded by **Kakadu National Park**, where Aboriginal people have lived for tens of thousands of years.
- **Terra Nullius ("nobody's land"):** the British legal construct — because Aboriginal people lacked the modern construct of "private property," the land was declared "unowned" and open to seizure.
- **"Development" vs. "wilderness" discourses** jointly shape what counts as "appropriate" land use. The operator **ERA** justifies mining via an "employment" discourse (claiming to be the Northern Territory's main Aboriginal employer), but Aboriginal people actually benefit little. The **Kakadu** dispute: environmentalists and Aboriginal people allied against the mine but disagreed on the park's purpose — the "wilderness" concept erases people from the land, while Aboriginal people want to protect the land *and* retain the right to enter it to make a living.

> **Half-life calculation template:** 100 g of C-14 (half-life 5730 yr) → after 1 half-life **50 g**, after 2 half-lives **25 g** (halves each half-life). Formula A = A₀e^(−λt).
> **Fission ≠ decay:** an isotope may be ① stable (¹²C, ¹³C); ② radioactive (¹⁴C); ③ fissile by neutron (²³⁵U); ④ fusible (stars, H-bomb). Fission example: U-235 + n → Ba-144 + Kr-90 + 2n + ~200 MeV.
> **Enrichment (memorize numbers):** natural uranium **0.7% U-235** / 99.3% mostly U-238; at 0.7% neutrons are too fast to sustain a chain reaction; **enriched to ~7%** fast neutrons can sustain a chain reaction and **at critical mass make a bomb.** **Reactor fuel is usually enriched to >4% U-235.**

#### Nuclear power & the three major accidents (★ commonly tested)
- **Nuclear power:** fission heat → boil water → steam drives a turbine → generator (**same principle as fossil-fuel power**; Einstein quipped nuclear power is "a hell of a way to boil water"). ~**16%** of global energy.
- **Reactor mechanism:** fuel = enriched U-235 pressed into pellets → stacked into fuel rods; **control rods** (neutron-absorbing) inserted among them — **pull out → reaction speeds up, insert → slows down.**
- **★ Three major accidents (in time order):**
  1. **Three Mile Island (28 Mar 1979, PA, USA):** a relief valve stuck open → core lost water → **partial meltdown**; **no major environmental release.**
  2. **Chernobyl (26 Apr 1986, Ukraine/USSR):** during a "how do backup pumps respond in a total blackout" test, **control rods were fully withdrawn** → runaway chain reaction → explosion destroyed containment, radioactive dust spread to the atmosphere. **Design flaws:** inadequate containment; **graphite** moderator (caught fire during meltdown, releasing more dust); a water pool beneath the reactor (a melt-through could trigger a larger explosion). Nearby worker-city **Pripyat permanently evacuated**; the **30-km exclusion zone** is still uninhabitable.
  3. **Fukushima (11 Mar 2011, Japan):** a massive earthquake + tsunami flooded the pump generators → core lost cooling and overheated → **meltdown.** Contaminated water leaked into the Pacific; top predators (bluefin tuna) showed trace radioactive dust (echoes the tuna chapter; a single serving's dose is <half an arm X-ray).
- **U.S. reactor design** uses **water as moderator → a self-stabilizing system** (hotter core → water vaporizes → reaction stops; cooler core → water densifies → reaction rises), unlike Chernobyl. The nuclear "Renaissance" was set back by these accidents.

#### The nuclear fuel chain & waste (detail)
- **★ The Nuclear Fuel Chain (every step has consequences):** ① **Mining** uranium ore; ② **Milling** — separate uranium into "**yellowcake**"; ③ **Conversion** — yellowcake to a **gas**; ④ **Enrichment** — separate fissile from non-fissile isotopes; ⑤ **Fuel fabrication** — pellets; ⑥ **Reactor** — fission heat + radioactive waste; ⑦ **Reprocessing or waste storage.** → Low carbon footprint is the selling point, but every step has human/environmental consequences, some catastrophic.
- **U.S. waste stockpile:** ~**100,000 tons low-level** waste + ~**15,000 tons high-level** waste (spent fuel); spent rods cooled in deep pools, then moved to metal-and-concrete casks beside the reactor.
- **Yucca Mountain three criteria (lecture version):** ① low moisture; ② geologically stable; ③ **far from major population centers.** **Maxey Flats (KY):** model said plutonium moves half an inch in 24,000 years; reality 2 miles in 10 years → long-term geological prediction can fail catastrophically.
- **Alternative disposal:** **transmutation** — use waste as fuel in another reactor, converting it to less-hazardous waste.
- **The fundamental paradox of uranium:** when all goes well it is extremely useful and provides relatively clean energy; but pollution, toxic waste, and potentially catastrophic consequences make its continued use hard to settle.

### 3.3 Mining & types of waste (R&F C7)
- **NIMBY (Not In My Back Yard):** the waste "solution" = put it in someone else's back yard, especially those without the political/economic/military power to stop it.
- **"Dilution is the solution to pollution" has failed:** many synthetic chemicals can't be broken down by microbes or oxygen and are toxic at very low concentrations.
- **Five waste types:**
  1. **Bulk** waste: household trash, scrapped cars, construction/demolition debris. OECD ~0.5 t/person/yr municipal (40% paper, ~10% each plastic/metal/glass). Three options: landfill / incineration (halves volume but may produce dioxins) / recycle-and-reduce. Landfills: liners, anaerobic decay produces **methane** (capturable as fuel), leachate.
  2. **Hazardous chemicals:** industrial chemicals, pesticides/herbicides (e.g., DDT, **non-biodegradable and persistent**).
  3. **Radioactive waste** (see uranium).
  4. **Biologically active waste:** sewage, fertilizer → **eutrophication** (excess nutrients).
  5. **Air pollution:** acid rain, smog (Module 2).
> **Boxes:** Bhopal gas leak (India, 1984); the asbestos panic (a counter-example of over-regulation — $100B+ spent removing mostly harmless asbestos); Fresh Kills landfill; cleaning up the Rhine; Brent Spar.

#### Week 8 supplement: gold-mining waste · mercury · cyanide · acid mine drainage (AMD)
> One-line theme: **pure gold itself is non-toxic, but the whole process of mining gold is extremely toxic.**

**A. Gold-mining basics**
- Most gold comes from **open-pit** mines. Humanity has mined ~**165,000 tons** of gold total (most still in circulation). **Gold grade is extremely low:** 1 ton (1 million g) of ore contains only a few grams of gold.
- **Carlin-type gold:** submicron gold in micron-scale arsenian pyrite, dispersed in carbonaceous shale/carbonate, **associated with Hg, Sb, As, U, Tl.** Top producers: China, Australia, Russia, USA.
- **Grasberg mine (Papua, Indonesia, 13,000 ft):** world's largest single gold reserve, 2nd-largest copper mine.

**B. ★ Environmental impacts of gold mining (high-frequency data)**
- **Earthworks estimate: producing the gold for one ring moves and discards ~20 tons of rock/earth.**
- Gold-mining waste carries **mercury (Hg) and cyanide** (used to extract gold from ore).
- Deep rock exposed to air and water → reactions form **sulfuric acid** → erodes and pollutes streams/rivers, ultimately reaching distant marine ecosystems.
- Gold mines release **hundreds of tons of elemental mercury** to the air yearly.
- EPA: **40% of western U.S. watershed headwaters** already polluted by mining; ~500,000 abandoned metal mines in 32 western states; cleanup may need **>$35 billion.**

**C. ★ The gold–mercury–arsenic toxicology link (signature concept)**
- On the periodic table, **Au and Hg are neighbors; As is a metalloid.** **Pure gold is non-toxic** (the U.S. ATSDR has no toxicological profile for gold), but ① extracting gold uses **highly toxic mercury** and ② gold ore commonly **concentrates the highly toxic metalloid arsenic.**
  - Line: "**Even if gold doesn't kill you, mining it will make you sicker, madder, and dumber**" (mercury → neural damage "madder"; arsenic → illness/cognitive damage "sicker and dumber").
  - Economic geologists long use **arsenic as a "pathfinder" element** for gold.
  - Cultural note: "Daiyu swallows gold" (*Dream of the Red Chamber*) — if gold is non-toxic, how does it kill? Answer: **physically, not chemically.**
- **Arsenic health effects (ATSDR):** an ancient known poison; large oral doses (water >60,000 ppb) are lethal; chronic oral exposure causes **arsenicosis** (skin lesions), skin cancer, and raises liver/bladder/lung cancer risk.
- **Mercury health effects (ATSDR):** the **nervous system** is extremely sensitive; the **kidneys** also; mercury vapor damages mouth/lungs; **methylmercury** harms the developing fetus (echoes §2.5).

**D. ★ Two gold-extraction methods and their hazards**
- **① Amalgamation (artisanal small-scale gold mining, ASGM):** mercury forms an amalgam with gold (~50% Hg + 50% Au) → **heat to evaporate the mercury, leaving gold** (mercury goes straight into the air/the operator's lungs).
  - ASGM is the livelihood of **~10–15 million miners in 70 countries** (including ~3 million women and children); it is **90% of the gold-mining workforce yet produces only 15% of the gold.**
  - **ASGM is the world's largest source of mercury demand:** ~**1400 tons** of mercury used in 2011; ~60% of miners use mercury.
  - **Porgera (PNG):** a large commercial mine (open since 1990); villages relocated; a Harvard/NYU 2010 report documented mercury exposure (one study N=351, children aged 7–12).
- **② Cyanide heap leach (for low-grade ore):** crush ore, heap on a leach pad → spray **cyanide** on top → gold leaches into the percolating solution → collect and recover. **Problem:** how to dispose of heaped tailings and cyanide? ~**1,785 tons of sodium cyanide** used globally per year; tailings have even been disposed of via **submarine tailing disposal.**
  - **Summitville (Colorado, Rio Grande headwaters):** cleanup has cost **$185 million** ($1.5M/yr), while the mine owner posted only a $3M bond — classic "privatize the profit, socialize the cost."

**E. ★ Acid Mine Drainage (AMD) (chemistry focus)**
- **Definition:** highly acidic water with high dissolved-metal concentrations, from surface/deep coal or metal mines and waste-rock piles; a major environmental problem in many mining areas.
- **Chemical mechanism (Singer & Stumm, 1970, *Science*)** — **Werner Stumm** (1924–1999) was the founder of aquatic chemistry.
  - **Reaction 1** (pyrite oxidation): **2FeS₂ + 7O₂ + 2H₂O → 4Fe²⁺ + 4SO₄²⁻ + 4H⁺.**
  - **Reaction 2** (ferrous oxidation): **4Fe²⁺ + O₂ + … → 4Fe³⁺** — this is the **rate-determining step** (Singer & Stumm 1970).
  - **Overall:** **4FeS₂ + 15O₂ + 14H₂O → 4Fe(OH)₃ + 8H₂SO₄.** Pyrite FeS₂ = "fool's gold"; product Fe(OH)₃ = amorphous ferrihydrite.
- **★ Reaction rates (key to the rate-determining step):**
  - Fe²⁺ oxidation by O₂ is **very slow** (1st/2nd order, strongly pH-dependent; half-life ~**1000 days** at pH<3).
  - Fe³⁺ oxidation of FeS₂ is **very fast** (half-life ~**50 minutes** at pH 1).
  - → **Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺ is the AMD bottleneck.** **Two ways to slow AMD:** ① slow the Fe²⁺ oxidation; ② remove Fe³⁺ from the system. Microbes (bacteria) and temperature both catalyze/accelerate the oxidation.
- **AMD treatment (Active vs. Passive):**
  - **Active:** physically dose alkali to **raise pH**; high cost, fast. Typical flow **"ODAS" = Oxidation → Dosing with alkali → Sedimentation.** Case: **Iron Mountain, California.**
  - **Passive:** use natural energy (wetlands, etc.); low maintenance, driven by volume.

**F. Ethics: should we mine new gold?** Gold mostly goes to **jewelry** (an industry society could use less of) — so **recycled gold could greatly reduce new mining.** But annual gold demand far exceeds the recycled supply, so new mining continues — "our greed for gold persists."

### 3.4 E-waste (RHM C17) ★ discussion 2 core
- **Digital Divide:** separates the consumers of electronics from those who "live with" e-waste.
- E-waste = discarded TVs, computers, phones; contains heavy metals (**lead, mercury, cadmium**) posing varied, often-unforeseen risks.
- **Planned Obsolescence** drives demand.
- **Markets lens: from externality to commodity:**
  - **Pollution Havens:** a systematic relationship — the **lower a country's per-capita GDP, the more it is a net importer** of e-waste.
  - Rich countries recycle some locally but ship vast amounts (legal + illegal) to Asia. Greenpeace: ~47% of waste exported from 18 European ports in 2005 was illegal; an estimated 50–80% of "recycled" U.S. waste is disposed of this way.
- **Basel Convention:** controls cross-border movement of hazardous waste. **The U.S. (the largest e-waste producer) has not ratified it**, making tracking hard.
- **Environmental-justice lens:**
  - **Guiyu (Guangdong):** called "the e-waste capital of the world"; tens of thousands of 150,000 residents do informal recycling (documented in BAN's 2003 film *Exporting Harm*); processes ~1.5 million lb/yr; heavy metals pollute air, water, food.
  - **Agbogbloshie (Ghana):** another famous dumping/recycling site.
  - These informal recyclers are **not passive victims** — this is dangerous but skilled work; huge surplus value is extracted from their labor while they provide the environmental service of handling waste others should handle themselves.

#### Week 9 supplement: e-waste "7 puzzles" + CRT lead + dioxins + national regulations
**★ RHM Ch.17 "The Puzzle of E-Waste":**
1. E-waste is an **anthropogenic hazard**, growing with TVs/PCs/phones and consumption.
2. E-waste's environmental/health **risks vary and are often unforeseen**, depending on the tech used to produce/process it.
3. The e-waste **market fluctuates** with demand and the price of "mineable" raw materials.
4. **Regulation becomes necessary** to prevent large-scale dumping on poorer countries.
5. Some firms find ways to turn e-waste **from an externality into a commodity.**
6. Despite regulation, **shadow recycling** grows alongside formal recycling → e-waste and its harms are **unevenly distributed** globally.
7. This unevenness **deepens environmental-justice problems.**

**A. What is e-waste + quantities**
- E-waste is a **small fraction** of the municipal waste stream but has **disproportionate** impact because: ① it contains **heavy metals and toxins**; ② it grows **fast**; ③ regulation/monitoring is inconsistent (no unified definition).
- **CRT/lead (memorize):** early TVs used **cathode-ray tubes (CRTs), each containing 4–8 lb of lead.** Leaded glass was once recycled, but after tech turnover (LCDs, etc.) **toxic leaded glass piled up**; new models contain *different* toxins.
- **Phones/elements:** phones are a small fraction by weight but **discarded in huge, growing numbers.** **Of 83 stable elements, ≥70 are found in smartphones** (up to ~60 per phone). 2014 global smartphone shipments: **1.3 billion** (+27.7% over 2013).
- **★ Global quantity:** ~**40 million tons/yr** of e-waste, only ~**13% recycled** (mostly in developing countries); the EU produces ~9 Mt. **Informal markets in China, India, Pakistan, Vietnam, the Philippines handle ~50–80%** — cutting, burning, dismantling in "backyards," emitting hazards.

**B. Risks (adding dioxins)**
- Three hazard sources: ① chemicals/metals/toxins **in the product**; ② chemicals released by **substandard processing**; ③ **reagents** used in recycling.
- **★ Dioxins (CDDs, products of burning chlorinated plastics):** chlorinated dibenzo-p-dioxins, a class of **75** compounds; the most toxic/studied is **2,3,7,8-TCDD** — causes **chloracne** and is listed as a **probable human carcinogen.**

**C. Markets (externality → commodity) & regulation**
- **★ CRT market collapse:** tech turnover killed demand for recycled CRT material → pileup and illegal dumping. **California's DTSC** then accepted CRTs into hazardous-waste sites (because that risk is lower than pileup/illegal dumping), approving emergency CRT regulations 15 Sep 2014 — **market failure still requires regulatory backstop.**
- **From externality to commodity:** e-waste is both hazard and **tradable commodity** (raw materials can be "mined"). E-waste management revenue: $9.15B (2011) → $20.25B (2016), ~17%/yr; **appliances ~46%** (largest source).
- **★ Regulations (compare):**
  - **EU WEEE Directive:** original took effect 2003, set a minimum target of **4 kg/person/yr** of recovered electronics; updated 2012 — because only ~1/3 of discarded equipment was compliantly handled.
  - **USA: no federal e-waste law** (repeated attempts failed); many **states** set mandatory recycling. **New York State** became the **23rd** state with e-waste recycling legislation on 28 May 2010 (requiring manufacturers to provide free, convenient recycling).
  - **China:** the *Regulations on Recycling and Disposal of Waste Electrical and Electronic Products* (announced 2009, amended 2019).
- **Recycling guidance/certification:** **reuse before recycle**; choose recyclers with **R2 and/or e-Steward certification** (echoes "green certification" — consumers can't verify, so rely on third-party certs). **Why recycle:** e-waste is both waste and resource; **recycling minerals may harm the environment less than new mining**; and it provides jobs.

**D. Pollution havens & political economy (environmental justice)**
- **★ Pollution Haven Hypothesis:** some countries **lower environmental standards to attract investment** → overall degradation worsens and waste flows to "the cheapest place" → environmental-justice problems.
- **★ Spatial Fix:** rich countries consume more electronics but send some waste to poor countries; **recycling** turns waste into commodity, keeping value circulating, thereby resolving over-accumulation.
- **Surplus value extracted:** even formal workers with some protection are paid below the value of their labor; informal recyclers are exposed at work and at home, yet the surplus value they create goes unacknowledged.
- **Global environmental justice:** the poor/marginalized are exposed to waste while affluent consumers are insulated; the **lower a country's per-capita GDP, the more it is a net importer.**

## MODULE 4 — Water: Safety & Security

> Natural science: R&F C4 (Water). Social science: RHM C15 (Bottled Water).

### 4.1 Water-resource basics (R&F C4)
- Water is the one resource you cannot live without: you can survive weeks without food but die in days without water.
- **★ Global water distribution (high-frequency data):**
  - ~**71% of the surface is covered by seawater;** oceans hold **97.5%** of all water.
  - **Only ~0.64% is liquid freshwater, and ~99% of that is groundwater.**
  - Rivers and freshwater lakes together are minuscule (rivers = 0.000002% of all water).
  - Conclusion: there is no *absolute* global water shortage; the problem is the **accessibility and uneven distribution** of freshwater (barring desalination/melting ice/condensation).
- Water is a **"fugitive" resource** — it crosses property and jurisdictional boundaries (a legal headache; echoes the market/Coase theorem).
- **Water budget / annual flux:** the total is roughly constant, but the volume flowing through the system each year (precipitation/evaporation/runoff — the **hydrologic cycle**) is large.
- **Uneven distribution:** too many people live in arid zones (Phoenix gets 7.66 in/yr — less than Damascus — yet has 1 million people).
- **Surface water:** rivers, lakes; **drainage basin/watershed**; dams. Cases: the Colorado ("a parable of a river," dry by its mouth), Aswan High Dam, Three Gorges.
- **Groundwater:** aquifer; **water table**; recharge. **High Plains (Ogallala) aquifer:** over-pumped far faster than recharge.
- **Boxes:** Owens Valley water war; **the shrinking Aral Sea** (Soviet diversion → desiccation, salt-dust storms, illness; now spans 5 countries, needs cooperation); the Everglades; the Jordan River; Libya's "Great Man-Made River."
- **★ Global water scarcity (high-frequency):** ~**1.2 billion** people live in **physical** water scarcity, another **500 million** approaching it; another **1.6 billion (~1/4 of humanity)** face **economic** water scarcity.
- **China's water situation:** per-capita available water ranks ~120th globally; projected ~500 m³ per capita by 2030.

### 4.2 Drinking-water safety & quality (R&F C4.5) ★ microbial-indicator lab core
- Groundwater is usually purer and needs little treatment; **almost all surface water needs purification** because upstream districts discharge (treated) sewage into rivers that downstream districts draw from. "A glass of New Orleans water has passed through at least 6 sewage systems."
- **Main drinking-water contaminants** (limits vary by use):
  - **① Bacteria & parasites (microbial) ★:** bacteria (typhoid/Salmonella, cholera, dysentery); parasites (amoebic dysentery, **bilharzia/schistosomiasis** — snails → skin penetration → urinary infection, common in Africa/Middle East). Ideal limit = zero. Disinfection mainly by **chlorination** (chlorine gas, or slow-release sodium hypochlorite = bleach) + filtration for large parasites; at home, **boiling** (kills all parasites and nearly all bacteria) or iodine tablets. Trace **fluoride** is often added to harden bones/teeth.
  - **② Chlorinated hydrocarbons (pesticides):** toxic, **non-biodegradable**, persistent (pesticides sprayed decades ago are still in water), usually <1 ppm; removable only by expensive distillation.
  - **③ Volatile hydrocarbons (gasoline, etc.):** usually only a few ppb; from oil spills, leaking gas-station tanks, dumping. Removable by repeated spraying/evaporation (expensive).
  - **④ Lead:** extremely dangerous to the nervous system, very low limit (0.01–0.02 ppb). Four anthropogenic sources: mine/smelter effluent, landfilled lead batteries, leaded-gasoline exhaust (mostly banned but residual), **leaded-solder water pipes.**
- **★ Microbial-indicator lab link:** test for **indicator organisms (E. coli / fecal coliform)** to judge fecal contamination. The indicator itself may not be pathogenic, but its presence implies possible pathogens — the standard method for assessing drinking-water safety.

#### Week 11 supplement: drinking-water quality — laws · MCL · watershed protection · eutrophication · Shenzhen
> Four framing questions: ① Where does your water come from? ② What's in it? ③ Where does it go after use? ④ Where will your children's water come from?

**A. ★ Two key laws: Clean Water Act vs. Safe Drinking Water Act (high-frequency, easily confused)**
- **Clean Water Act (CWA, 1972):** aims to make U.S. **surface water** "**fishable and swimmable**"; governs pollution **discharged into rivers/lakes/seas** (the health of the *water body*).
- **Safe Drinking Water Act (SDWA, 1974, amended 1996):** governs the health of **tap water (public supply) at the point of delivery**; EPA sets drinking-water contaminant limits under it. (Basis for §4.3: bottled water → FDA, tap water → EPA and stricter.)
- **One-line distinction:** **CWA protects the "water body" (ecology/swimmable); SDWA protects the "water you drink" (human health).**

**B. ★ MCL & the National Primary Drinking Water Regulations (NPDWR)**
- **Maximum Contaminant Level (MCL):** a *legally enforceable* limit for public water systems, protecting public health; EPA has set MCLs for ~**87 contaminants.**
- **★ Six regulated categories (be able to list):** ① **Microorganisms** (e.g., coliforms); ② **Disinfectants** (e.g., chlorine); ③ **Disinfection Byproducts** (e.g., trihalomethanes); ④ **Inorganic Chemicals** (e.g., arsenic); ⑤ **Organic Chemicals** (e.g., PCBs); ⑥ **Radionuclides** (e.g., uranium, radium).
- Note the inherent trade-off: disinfection (kills ① microbes) itself **produces ③ disinfection byproducts** (e.g., trihalomethanes) — why NYC's Croton system needs a new filtration plant (see E).

**C. ★ Public supply vs. private wells (the key U.S. regulatory gap)**
- **Urban/public supply:** mostly **surface water** (e.g., NYC), **must meet MCLs.**
- **Rural/private wells:** ~**half the U.S. population and 95% of rural residents drink groundwater**; but **private-well water is not subject to MCLs** and has no mandatory testing — the biggest rural drinking-water hazard.
- **★ Natural arsenic in private-well water:** **high-arsenic groundwater is a global problem** (Smedley & Kinniburgh 2002, USGS); the **arsenic MCL = 10 µg/L (10 ppb).** ~100,000 people in New England private wells are exposed to >10 µg/L natural arsenic; a Maine survey found ~31% of wells >10 µg/L. → Unregulated wells + natural arsenic = invisible long-term exposure.

**D. ★ Point vs. nonpoint sources**
- **Point sources:** a single locatable outfall — sewage plants, factories.
- **Nonpoint sources:** diffuse, hard to locate — urban street runoff, suburban development, farm fields, feedlots, rural households. Nonpoint is **harder to regulate** and the core challenge of watershed protection.

**E. ★ New York City water supply — a world-class "watershed protection" model (positive institutions case)**
- NYC supply: **3 unfiltered surface sources** (**Catskill, Delaware, Croton**, ~**2000 sq mi** of watershed) + 1 groundwater source (Jamaica, Queens); reservoirs hold ~550 billion gallons; ~**1.3 billion gallons/day.**
- **Filtration Waiver:** the Catskill/Delaware systems earned a **federal filtration waiver** thanks to strong source protection — internationally recognized as a **model of watershed protection** (saving the cost of a filtration plant); the older Croton system is being evaluated for a filtration plant under stricter disinfection-byproduct rules.
- **★ NYC Watershed MOA (be able to describe its measures):** ① **agricultural program** — move riparian-wetland farms away from water bodies; ② **land acquisition** — plan to buy **355,000 acres** of sensitive land; ③ **regulations** — activities affecting water quality require permits; ④ **partnership program** — invest **$280M** to repair septic systems, stormwater, etc.; ⑤ **sewage-plant upgrades** — all to advanced treatment.
- Lesson: rather than build a filtration plant (end-of-pipe), **protect the watershed at the source** (front-end + institutional prevention) — institutional design beats technological remediation.

**F. ★ The enforcement gap (echoes institutions / political economy)**
- **NY Times 2009 (Charles Duhigg), "Clean Water Laws Are Neglected, at a Cost in Suffering":** of 4,606 NY facilities, 856 had violations 2004–2007, but penalties were mostly **$0** — **having a law ≠ enforcing it.** (*What causes weak CWA enforcement, and what to do?* → insufficient regulatory resources/political will, penalties too weak to deter, opacity; remedies = stronger enforcement, higher penalties, open data, public oversight.)

**G. Where does used water go? Sewage treatment**
- NYC's **14 sewage plants** treat **1.3 billion gallons/day** (a **combined** sewer system: sewage + rain/snow runoff).
- **Effluent still carries lots of nitrogen** (even after treatment) → causes **eutrophication** in the bay; further nitrogen removal is **technically feasible but very expensive** (*given that sewage fees already exceed water fees, is nitrogen removal economically feasible?* — a classic "who bears the cost" trade-off).

**H. ★ Eutrophication & "dead zones"**
- **Definition (Jorgensen & Richardson 1996):** the process of altering a water body's trophic state by increasing nutrient input; features = greatly human-accelerated, algal proliferation, water-quality decline, ecosystem disruption.
- **Nitrogen sources:** **industrial nitrogen fixation (mainly fertilizer)**, nitrogen-fixing crops, fossil-fuel combustion — coastal eutrophication exploded from the mid-20th century (Boesch 2002).
- **Consequences:** hypoxia ("dead zones") → loss of benthic/planktonic invertebrates and fish. **Famous dead zones:** the **Gulf of Mexico** (Mississippi drainage), **Chesapeake Bay, Long Island Sound**; globally also the NW Black Sea, N Adriatic, Yellow Sea, East China Sea.

**I. Health: improved sanitation (global inequality)**
- 1990–2010: ~2 billion gained improved water, 1.8 billion improved sanitation; but ~**780 million** still lack improved drinking water and **2.5 billion** lack improved sanitation (UNICEF/WHO JMP 2012).
- Infant mortality is strongly *negatively* correlated with per-capita GDP; **open defecation** is far higher among the poorest than the richest.

**J. ★ Shenzhen local case (contrast with NYC)**
- **Basics:** subtropical maritime-monsoon climate, ~**1830 mm/yr** rainfall (85% in Apr–Sep); 330 rivers, 168 reservoirs; **per-capita water <200 m³, ~1/11 of the national average** — scarce local water + frequent typhoon storms + serious river pollution.
- **Source protection:** 32 important drinking-water sources, 27 protection zones, ~**594 km²** total (primary/secondary/quasi-protection) — analogous to NYC's watershed protection.
- **★ Standard upgrade (GB 5749-2006 replacing the 1985 version):** indicators rose from **35 to 106**; microbial indicators from **2 to 6** (adding E. coli, thermotolerant coliforms, **Giardia, Cryptosporidium**); disinfectants from 1 to 4.
- **2017 report:** 11 centralized sources monitored, **all met or exceeded Surface-Water Class III (100%)**, 9 reached Class II.
- **New problems:** ~15% of sewage untreated (2016); **serious antibiotic pollution in Shenzhen rivers.** Future direction: **"Water-Smart Cities"** — riverbank filtration (RBF), soil-aquifer treatment (SAT), precise monitoring + smart early warning.
- **Information gap:** *Why is Shenzhen's water-quality data-sharing insufficient, and how to improve?* → open-data mechanisms, a unified platform, public accessibility (echoing NYC's published annual water-quality reports).
- **Aside:** bottled water **$3/gal** vs. NYC tap **$0.002/gal** (~1500×).

### 4.3 Bottled water — commodifying the "free commons" (RHM C15) ★ discussion 3 core
- **★ "A Tale of Two Bottles" (memorize):**
  - **Luisa (Tijuana, Mexico):** ~25% of residents are "off-grid" with no tap water; she hauls 40+ lb of 5-gal water daily at ~10 pesos (~$1)/5 gal — water that is mostly drawn straight from the municipal supply. Tijuanans' scarcity makes them among the world's most water-efficient people.
  - **Carlos (San Diego, USA):** has free 24-hour tap water yet buys Dasani at $1.49/16 oz (≈ $60/5 gal), ~30 gal/yr.
  - **Key irony:** the two are drinking **the same Colorado River water!** San Diego's bottled water just wears a Coca-Cola blue label and sells for **60× the Mexican equivalent** and **hundreds of times its own tap-water source.**
- **Core question:** how does a free-flowing, life-essential substance become a captured, marketed, globally distributed commodity? Does bottled-water marketing reflect real scarcity, legitimate health risk, or a marketing executive's trick?
- **Brief history:** spa "health" waters (1700s) → 19th-c. science debunked the claims → Poland Spring (mid-1800s, elite luxury) → Evian/Perrier (1970s) → explosive growth from the 1990s.
- **Types:** spring/artesian, purified (reverse osmosis — often filtered municipal tap), mineral, enhanced.

#### Week 10 supplement: bottled water "6 puzzles" + three scarcities + FDA vs EPA + risk-perception bias
**★ RHM Ch.15 "The Puzzle of Bottled Water":**
1. Bottled-water consumption has grown **explosively**, starting to **compete with and replace** other water sources.
2. Producing/packaging/transporting bottled water is **not environmentally friendly.**
3. Meeting water needs in a fast-growing, urbanizing world is hard; but bottled water counts as a response to scarcity **only if we recognize different kinds of scarcity** (hydrological/socioeconomic/perceptual).
4. A core driver is the **perception that other sources aren't safe/healthy enough.**
5. **Risk assessment shows bottled water has no significant advantage** over tap — raising questions about **risk-perception bias.**
6. The market grows in **both poor and rich countries**, hinting at a common economic driver — the **commodification of nature** (privatization of water).

**A. Growth, price, environmental footprint**
- **Price gap:** bottled water can be **>1000×** the price of tap (e.g., $3/gal vs. $0.002/gal).
- **Plastic/BPA:** bottles often labeled **PET**, marketed "BPA-free." **BPA** (in polycarbonate and epoxy resins) is an **endocrine disruptor** — "are we swapping one fear (tap) for another (plastic)?"
- **★ Carbon-footprint LCA (Siena, Italy study):** per **1.5 L**, **bottled = 3.37×10⁻¹ kg CO₂eq, tap = 1.35×10⁻³ kg CO₂eq** (bottled ~**250×** tap). Impacts come from fossil fuels in bottle-making/transport and bottles discarded/landfilled.

**C. ★ Three kinds of water scarcity (full version)**
| Type | Cause | Relievable by money/tech? | Typical region |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hydrological (physical)** | Climate/hydrology causes shortage | Yes (bottled water, desalination — expensive) | Deserts, e.g., **UAE** |
| **Techno-economic** | Lacks pipe infrastructure or treatment capacity | Common in fast-urbanizing poor regions | **Kolkata, Dhaka, Mexico City** |
| **Perceptual** | Drinkable water exists but is *felt* to be scarce/unsafe | Psychological/marketing | **Europe, USA** |
- One-line link: reasons to buy bottled water differ by place — **not enough water (hydrological) / not enough clean water (techno-economic) / *feeling* there isn't enough clean water (perceptual).** The first two are real scarcity; the third leads to puzzles 4&5 on "risk perception."

**D. ★ Is bottled water really safer? (regulation + risk perception, commonly tested)**
- **★ Regulatory asymmetry (high-frequency):** **bottled water is regulated by the FDA with looser standards; tap water by the EPA under the SDWA (1974) with stricter standards.**
- **★ NRDC 1999 study (signature evidence):** tested 103 brands, >1000 bottles from several states — **about 1/3 of bottled waters** contained **significant contamination** in at least one test (exceeding a state or industry standard); 4% violated the (already weak) FDA federal bottled-water standard; 8% had **arsenic** at health-concern levels. **Conclusion: there is no guarantee that bottled water is safer than tap.**
- **★ Risk-perception bias (echoes the risk lens):** what people fear and what is actually dangerous often diverge. Ranked by "years cut from average U.S. lifespan": **poverty 7–10 yr > being male 7.5 yr > smoking/overweight 6 yr > … > air pollution 5 months > … > living next to a nuclear plant for life only 10 hours.** → The public over-fears low-probability hazards (nuclear, toxic waste, bottled-vs-tap) and underestimates high-impact ones (poverty, smoking, obesity). Pair with **dose-response** ("the dose makes the poison").

**E. ★ Political economy — manufactured demand & the enclosed commons**
- **Primitive Accumulation:** water is **appropriated, turned from a common resource into a commodity, and sold back at a profit.** Privatizing water in poor countries deprives many of access to clean, affordable water.
- **Overproduction / Manufactured Demand:** firms overproduce → must use **marketing and packaging to manufacture demand** → **get the rich to pay for what they could get (nearly) free.** This is "commodification of nature" applied to bottled water.

## MODULE 5 — Food

> Natural science: R&F C2 (Food). Social science: RHM C13 (Tuna), C16 (French Fries).
> Two "objects": **Tuna (§5.2, RHM C13, Week 16)** and **French Fries (§5.3, RHM C16, Week 15).** Both put "green consumption/certification" under a critical microscope. (Note: Week 15 = fries, Week 16 = tuna — reversed from the chapter order C13→C16; no conflict.)

### 5.1 Key elements of food production (R&F C2)
#### Human dietary requirements
- Essentials: water, **carbohydrates** (energy; glucose/starch; cellulose = indigestible fiber for the gut), **fats** (saturated vs. unsaturated), **proteins** (amino acids; essential vs. non-essential), **minerals** (calcium, iron, iodine), **special chemicals** (hormones, enzymes, vitamins).
- **Vitamins:** water-soluble (B, C) vs. fat-soluble (A, D, E, K). Vitamin D is actually a hormone (sunlight on skin cholesterol); **B₁₂ is found only in animal products**; scurvy = vitamin-C deficiency.
- **Iodine → thyroid hormone**, so industrial societies iodize salt.
- **Ruminants** (cattle, sheep) have a rumen where microbes break down cellulose — humans can't.

#### The three requirements for crop production (★ "key elements")
**Plants need: light, water, nutrients.**
- **Light:** plants absorb only ~1–2% of incident solar radiation; 2 t/acre of wheat uses ~0.1%.
  - **C3 vs. C4 photosynthesis:** most temperate crops are C3 (close stomata at hot midday, poor CO₂ uptake); **C4 plants (corn, sugarcane)** photosynthesize in heat with low CO₂ and grow faster.
- **Water:** producing 1 t of wheat needs ~**500 t of water**; good farmland needs ~40 in/yr rain or irrigation. **~80% of U.S. water goes to irrigation.**
- **Nutrients:** plants get carbon from air, water from below, **and almost everything else from soil fertility.**
  - Soil forms from weathered rock (A/B horizons); **tropical soils are nutrient-poor** due to heavy leaching.
  - **Key fertilizer elements = nitrogen N, phosphorus P, potassium K.** **Phosphorus comes from phosphate rock (a finite mineral resource)** — linking food to the minerals module. Nitrogen fixation: legumes (e.g., soybeans).
- **Grains** (wheat, corn, rice) provide >50% of global calories.
- **Energy efficiency / trophic levels (important critique):** feeding grain to livestock then eating the meat is **highly inefficient** (huge energy loss per trophic level). ~**69% of U.S. consumed grain feeds cattle**; poor countries (Tanzania, India) feed almost none to cattle (India: cattle are sacred in Hinduism).
- **Food aid & trade:** rich countries (US, Canada, W. Europe) can export; poor countries trade other products for grain or rely on aid. **The value and ethics of food aid are deeply contested** (e.g., Box 2.1: how the great powers ruined Somali agriculture).

### 5.2 Tuna — a microcosm of the ocean crisis (RHM C13) ★ discussion 4 core
#### Science & history
- Tuna are in the family Scombridae; **endothermic (warm-blooded)** (fast muscle oxygenation); swim near 70 mph; **migrate 4000+ miles → only a truly global effort can protect them.**
- **★ Cross-link to "mercury in fish" (§2.5-D):** tuna are large, high-trophic fish, so **methylmercury bioaccumulates** in them — coal Hg → ocean methylation → big-fish concentration → human (esp. pregnant) intake. This is where Week 1's "mercury in fish" question, Week 2-2's "coal and sushi" hook, and this chapter converge.
- Main commercial species: **bluefin** (largest; belly "toro" for sushi/sashimi), **yellowfin**, bigeye, albacore (canned "white"), skipjack.
- **Bluefin's reversal of fortune:** once called "horse mackerel," considered inedible (pet food); from the 1960s, Japanese refrigerated fleets + soaring sushi demand → prices soared (one fish auctioned for $32,000), overfishing.
- **Tuna "ranching":** **purse-seine** entire schools of juveniles, tow to coastal pens, fatten for months, then export (mainly to Japan). **Regulatory vacuum:** not counted in catch quotas (though permanently removed from the wild) and not subject to aquaculture rules; removing juveniles wrecks the population age structure → further destroys wild bluefin.
- **Overfishing:** catch > regeneration. Consequences both ecological and social — **small-scale "artisanal" fishers are hit first and hardest** (big firms can move elsewhere; small fishers can't). Many common supermarket fish are already badly overfished (Atlantic cod — Canadian moratorium 1992; orange roughy; hammerhead sharks).

#### Markets lens: the "dolphin-safe" label (★ green-consumption "success story" and critique)
- Background: in the Eastern Tropical Pacific (ETP) yellowfin fishery, yellowfin swim below dolphin schools → **dolphins die as bycatch** in purse seines.
- Legislative attempts: **Marine Mammal Protection Act (MMPA, 1972)** (had loopholes; dolphins kept dying); 1976 amendment (require driving dolphins out, "Medina panel" fine mesh); a 1984 amendment even allowed the U.S. fleet to kill 20,500 dolphins/yr.
- **Consumer action:** **Earth Island Institute (EII)** launched a canned-tuna **boycott**; the 1988 LaBudde film helped. In 1990 the three big U.S. brands (**StarKist, Chicken of the Sea, Bumble Bee, 90% of the market**) pledged to buy only "**Dolphin Safe**" tuna; government legislation + trade embargo (against Mexico/Venezuela/Panama). Dolphin mortality fell ~**99%** from its 1960s peak.
- **Critique:** how many consumers truly know the "dolphin safe" definition? How many wrongly assume "dolphin safe" = "eco-friendly" (a dubious assumption)? Green labels proliferate and ordinary consumers can't verify each certification — the **greenwashing / certification-reliability** problem.

#### Week 16 supplement: tuna "8 puzzles" + lead/Patterson + MSY/EEZ + MSC + ethics
> The lecture opens with **lead pollution** to set the tone (tuna concentrate both mercury and, historically, lead), then unfolds 8 puzzles. Core tension = **market environmentalism ("green consumption makes firms do right") vs. political economy ("capital accumulation is the root; markets can never save us")**, landing on **ethics**: "the dolphins won, the tuna and the ocean lost."

**★ Opening: Settle & Patterson (1980, *Science*), lead in canned tuna**
- The paper *Lead in Albacore* (Science 207:1167–1176): canned tuna had lead **10,000×** natural concentrations, ignored for decades due to analytical error; modern U.S. diets 0.2 ppm lead vs. prehistoric <0.002 ppm.
- **Clair C. Patterson (1922–1995, Caltech geochemist):** used U-Pb / Pb-Pb dating to compute **Earth's age at 4.55 billion years (1956, still essentially unchanged)**; his lead-pollution work **directly drove the bans on tetraethyl lead in gasoline and leaded solder in food cans** — a model of how one scientist can move public policy.
- **Lead is a neurotoxin:** harms IQ (Needleman 1990); even low blood-lead is harmful. → Like mercury, tuna is a vector for "invisible population-wide toxic exposure."

**★ RHM Ch.13 "The 8 Puzzles":**
1. Tuna exist in almost unimaginable numbers, **yet are unsustainably harvested nearly everywhere.**
2. Wild stocks are hit by **multiple new fishing technologies** (purse seiners, ranching), each pulling out more fish.
3. Tuna **ignore political boundaries**, yet their fate is bound to **geopolitical decisions.**
4. Tuna fetch **high prices**; when big money is at stake, ecological considerations get shelved.
5. In the ETP, yellowfin swim below easily spotted dolphin schools, so **dolphins died for cheap canned tuna ("Blood Tuna")** for years.
6. **Markets view:** green consumption "re-regulated" tuna production via the dolphin-safe label — markets + consumer advocacy can make firms "do the right thing."
7. **Political-economy view:** conversely, critically scrutinize market solutions — watch the **shifted sites and scales of power** behind each ("green") market success.
8. **Ethics view:** at least in the oceans, **our ecological ethics has not caught up with our support for animal rights.**

**A. ★ Puzzle 3: geopolitics — MSY & EEZ**
- **Maximum Sustainable Yield (MSY):** the largest amount of a resource that can be harvested indefinitely. But this "ceiling" spawns a **"race to harvest"** — whoever catches first wins, accelerating depletion (echoes the tragedy of the commons).
- **Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ):** a **200-nautical-mile** offshore zone for managing a nation's fish. **Problem:** tuna **migrate across/beyond EEZ boundaries** (Atlantic bluefin spawn in the Mediterranean and Gulf of Mexico; managed as two units by **ICCAT**) — no single nation can manage a fugitive global commons (echoes §2.4 climate and §4.1 fugitive resources).
- **New mechanism ISSF:** International Seafood Sustainability Foundation (2009, scientists + industry + NGOs), using science to promote long-term tuna conservation and reduce bycatch.

**D. ★ Puzzle 8: ethics — "the social construction of charismatic species" + "dolphins win, tuna lose" (signature)**
- **Ecological Ethic vs. Animal-Rights Ethic:** the former holds **killing individual animals is acceptable as long as the species isn't threatened** (focus on system/species); the latter holds **animals can suffer and so should have rights, and any act causing suffering is wrong** (focus on the individual).
- **★ Social Construction of Charismatic Species:** dolphins are constructed as "**charismatic species** with interests humans value," while tuna are constructed as "**just fish/food**" — so conservation resources skew heavily toward marine mammals. **But species-biased animal rights can cause *greater* ecological damage.**
- **★ "The dolphins' victory, the tuna's (and many species') defeat":** once all U.S.-sold tuna is "dolphin safe," boats switched methods — ① **"log sets"** kill fewer dolphins **but produce 2–3× more bycatch of other marine species**; ② **moving to dolphin-free grounds** caused **rapid overexploitation** there. → This green certification only guaranteed "fewer dolphins killed," while making tuna fishing *overall* less sustainable.
- **★ WTO 2012 ruling:** after full scientific/empirical review, the U.S.'s 1990 "dolphin safe" standard was found **outdated and actually deceptive to U.S. consumers** — an authoritative indictment of green-label credibility.
- Soul-question: "**Why does no one care about the rights of tuna?**" "**Dolphin-safe is not tuna safe. We still eat tuna. Lots of it.**"

**E. ★ Puzzles 6&7: markets vs. political economy (argue both sides)**
- **Market Environmentalism:** markets can solve environmental problems — the dolphin-safe label proves consumer advocacy can push firms toward good (puzzle 6).
- **Political Economy:** capitalist production *itself* is the root, so markets can never solve it (puzzle 7). It exposes capital accumulation's pressure on environment and society, and how tuna are exploited toward extinction.
- **★ Second contradiction of capitalism (via tuna):** changing the means of production (purse seines) + fierce competition **threaten the conditions of production (the tuna themselves)** — **"the more successful the fishing, the fewer fish in the sea."** Without global capitalism, boats and nets might never have been upgraded; precisely because the global market made tuna fishing so profitable, technology iterated madly; and **workers who don't own the means of production** won't oppose the very change that ultimately destroys the industry and their jobs.
- **★ New certification MSC (Marine Stewardship Council):** a **post-Fordist** regulatory system; compliant products get the MSC label. **Critique (echoes certification proliferation): obtaining certification requires money and connections, so small firms and individual fishers are often excluded** — the "green" threshold is itself unfair.
- **"Will you pay more for sustainability?"** Bumble Bee albacore ~**$1.42/can** vs. Wild Selections sustainable ~**$3.60/can** — green consumption ultimately rests on the **consumer's willingness to pay** (echoes the limits of "voting with dollars").

### 5.3 French fries — a microcosm of the industrial food system (RHM C16) ★ Week 15
> Opens with **Michael Pollan**: "**Eat food. Not too much. Mostly plants.**" (*In Defense of Food*, 2009) — setting the "rethinking industrial diets" tone.

**★ RHM Ch.16 "The 6 Puzzles":**
1. Fries, like other foods, have a **history spanning centuries and continents.**
2. Large-scale fry production is inseparable from the **rise and spread of fast food from the 1950s.**
3. Not just any potato makes fries — large-scale frozen fries need a variety **widely growable and suited to commodity agriculture**; under heavy **chemical, water, energy** inputs, **Russet Burbank is the perfect fry potato.**
4. The **risk lens** can focus on the human scale — what we eat depends partly on which negative consequences we associate with it; risk judgments are shaped by social context (including the production of scientific knowledge).
5. The **political-economy lens** shows eating fries **is not the purely individual choice it appears to be.**
6. The **ethics lens** stresses the biodiversity cost of Russet Burbank monoculture; we might reintroduce lost potato species to protect ecology, or use **biotechnology (GM)** to improve Russet Burbank — a deeply contested tool.

**A. Fry history & fast food**
- **Belgian or French?** Belgians may have first fried thin potato strips in the Meuse valley in the late 17th c.; the name "French" is disputed.
- **France's potato champion Parmentier:** in 1748 the French parliament banned potatoes (believed to cause leprosy); army pharmacist **Antoine-Augustin Parmentier** promoted them after the Seven Years' War; the Paris faculty of medicine declared potatoes edible in 1772; they truly caught on only after the **1785 famine.**
- **"The American century of the fry":** WWI soldiers brought fried potatoes home; drive-throughs + fast food made fries ubiquitous. Today in the U.S.: **McDonald's is the largest potato buyer**; **90% of consumed potatoes are fast food**; **90% of frozen-potato products are fries.**

**B. ★ Puzzle 3: the perfect fry potato Russet Burbank (specimen of high-input agriculture)**
- **Potato origin & spread:** from the **Lake Titicaca region** of the Andes (Peru/Bolivia), domesticated ~7,000–10,000 years ago; the Inca prized it for food security (can be freeze-dried); introduced to Spain 1532–1572, **reaching Europe ~1570**; prized by sailors for preventing **scurvy.** Spread worldwide via the **Columbian Exchange.**
- **★ The Irish Potato Famine (the painful lesson of monoculture dependence):** by the mid-19th c. Ireland over-depended on a **single variety, the lumper**; **three years of late-blight crop failure, 1845–1848** → **1.5 million+ dead or emigrated.** (Direct evidence for puzzle 6: monoculture = biodiversity risk.)
- **Russet Burbank itself:** bred by **Luther Burbank (1849–1926)** over 55 years, released in the 1920s; **large, uniform, with just-right sugar content for frying** → the world's most dominant potato.
- **★ The fresh→processed consumption shift (1960s → 2000s):** fresh 81→**42 lb/person**; processed 43→**86 lb/person** (frozen — mainly fries — 55 lb/person). **From 1970, processed potato consumption surpassed fresh** — the turning point of industrial diets.
- **★ The cost of growing Russet Burbank (echoes "no free lunch"):** ① the most dominant fry potato; ② potatoes are a high-risk crop (whole-season failures); ③ it is **not easy to grow — needing ever more inputs: pesticides, fertilizer, water, energy** (the lecture names the insecticide carbofuran).

**C. ★ Puzzle 4: risk lens — fries & the human body (obesity & trans fat)**
- **Obesity data:** over 30 years, childhood obesity doubled and adolescent obesity quadrupled; ages 6–11 from 7% (1980) to ~18% (2012), ages 12–19 from 5% to ~21%; in 2012 over 1/3 of children/adolescents were overweight or obese.
- **★ The trans-fat story (how risk perception is shaped by science and time):** **hydrogenation** makes oils more stable and palatable but produces trans fat. **In 1957 the AHA linked saturated fat to heart disease** → trans fat was briefly hailed as a "healthy substitute"; **in the 1990s the AHA linked trans fat to heart disease, diabetes, etc.** Butter (animal fat, cholesterol + high saturated fat) vs. margarine (vegetable oil, no cholesterol, but harder = more trans fat).
- **Risk, choice, regulation:** despite known harm, fast food still fried fries in trans fat and people still ate it — **because trans-fat harm is delayed and non-catastrophic, the risk is easily underestimated** (echoes risk-perception bias). Does "**better information**" yield "**better choices**"?
- **★ Regulatory action (a command-and-control example):** **NYC was first to ban restaurant trans fats (2006)**; **the FDA preliminarily determined partially hydrogenated oils (PHO) were no longer "Generally Recognized As Safe (GRAS)" in Nov 2013** — a gradual national phase-out.

**D. ★ Puzzle 5: political economy — "Eat Fries or Else!"**
- **Core thesis:** socioeconomic conditions **greatly constrain which choices a person can make** — what we eat is far from pure individual free will.
- **Marketing & food choice:** food is both cultural product and cultural identity; marketing leverages culture to make fast food succeed; **branding** competes by differentiation (Big Mac vs. Whopper) or cost-cutting; **changing how we perceive food (e.g., the "supersize" portion norm) raises consumption and profit.**
- **★ Food Desert (environmental-justice dimension, high-frequency):** areas with **far higher fast-food than grocery/produce density**; common in **low-income and minority communities**; correlated with high diabetes/obesity. Over **23 million Americans (incl. 6.5 million children) live in food deserts** (>1 mile from a supermarket). → "Choice" is bounded by the geography of supply — the heart of the political-economy lens.

**E. ★ Puzzle 6: ethics — preserve or alter the potato's heritage**
- **Biodiversity cost:** chasing the "perfect potato" created some new varieties and **lost many.** The potato (*Solanum tuberosum*, family Solanaceae) has **4,500+ native varieties** (mostly Andean) + 100–180 **wild species** (bitter and inedible but holding valuable disease/stress-resistance genes); it is the **world's 3rd-largest food crop** (after rice and wheat), eaten by over a billion people.
- **Ecocentric vs. anthropocentric (the ethical opposition):** **ecocentric** — we are wiping out other potato species and the organisms that depend on them; **anthropocentric** — meeting human needs matters more; but a **reverse anthropocentric** argument also holds: **reducing potato genetic diversity plants the seed of the next "late blight"** (the ghost of the Irish famine).
- **★ Preservation vs. Conservation vs. Land Ethic (distinguish):** Peru built a **national potato registry / "Potato Park"** (CIP gene bank) to preserve varieties. **Why preserve?** — **Preservation** = protecting potatoes **for their own sake** (from destruction); **Conservation** = protecting them **for human use.** **Aldo Leopold's "Land Ethic"** frames such efforts as **sustainable agriculture** between the two.
- **★ GM potato (a contested tool):** **New Leaf** is a GM Russet Burbank that **produces its own insecticide** to prevent failure, **abandoned due to consumer resistance**; patented GM crops also raise concerns — **farmers forced to buy new seed yearly while the patent holder keeps profiting** (a seed-sovereignty / capital-accumulation critique). → Ethical question: **reintroduce lost species** to protect ecology, or use **biotechnology** to alter the monoculture?

| ISSF | International Seafood Sustainability Foundation (2009); an industry + scientist + NGO fisheries-sustainability body. |
| Maguro / Akami / Toro | Tuna / lean meat / fatty belly meat (sushi terms); toro (belly fat) is priciest. |
| ★ Russet Burbank | McDonald's flagship fry potato; Andean-origin, spread via the Columbian Exchange; high-input monoculture. |
| Late blight | The potato disease behind the Irish famine (1845–48); the fatal weakness of a single variety (the lumper). |
| Columbian Exchange | The swap of species between Old and New Worlds (potato from the Americas to Europe). |
| Solanaceae | The nightshade family the potato belongs to (incl. tomato, eggplant, tobacco). |
| ★ Food desert | An area lacking affordable healthy food; ~23 million people, mostly low-income/minority (political-economy + risk lens). |
| Trans fat / PHO | Produced by hydrogenation; linked to cardiovascular disease in the 1990s; NYC banned it in 2006, the FDA ruled PHO not "GRAS" in 2013. |
| Hydrogenation | The process that solidifies liquid vegetable oil, producing trans fat. |
| GM crop / New Leaf | Monsanto's insect-resistant GM potato, dropped after market resistance (ethics lens). |
| Preservation vs conservation | Preservation = protect without use; conservation = wise use (the ethics-lens dichotomy). |
| Land ethic (Leopold) | Leopold: extend the moral community to soils, waters, plants and animals; ecocentrism. |

# PART 4 — DEBATE & DISCUSSION PREP (4 discussions + 1 debate)

The discussions/debate in this course put an **object** through several **lenses**. Use this template every time: **① What is the scientific fact? ② How does each lens analyse it? ③ My position + the other side's strongest argument + my reply.**

## The CO₂ debate (Week 4)
- **Scientific consensus (the shared ground both sides accept):** IPCC AR5 — humans are "extremely likely" the main cause; AR6 — "unequivocal"; 2011–2020 already **+1.1 °C**; CO₂ now **~428 ppm (Jan 2026)**; carbon budget ~2900 Gt with ~65% used; holding to 2 °C needs a 40–70% cut by 2050, holding to 1.5 °C needs net zero.
- **Pro side (cut/regulate hard → leans institutions + political economy):** climate is the free-rider problem of the largest global commons, so it needs international institutions (Kyoto 1997/2005, Paris 2015); the political-economy view says capital accumulation is the root and "the market has only concentrated wealth, not cut carbon", so it favours **regulatory** solutions; cutting emissions is "affordable" (it only slows growth by ~0.06% — a delay, not a sacrifice).
- **Sceptic/market side (markets/development → leans market efficiency):** externalities should be internalised by the market, and a **carbon tax beats cap-and-trade** (predictable price, fast to implement, revenue-neutral, economy-wide — vs cap-and-trade's historical-allocation "windfalls" and its coverage of only ~40% (electricity)); developing countries have a right to develop (U.S. per-capita emissions are 5× India's, historical responsibility 3× China's); green consumption/offsets have limits (greenwashing, offsets may not truly offset).
- **"Analyse carbon through three lenses" answer skeleton:** institutions (commons → Kyoto/Paris/who pays) | markets (externality → carbon tax vs cap-and-trade → Shenzhen ETS) | political economy (capital accumulation → doubt market fixes, favour regulation).

## Discussion 1: Uranium / nuclear power (Week 7)
- **Core questions:** Is nuclear a wise way forward? Who benefits, who is harmed? What is the long-term solution for the waste? (Apply the 6 puzzles of the uranium chapter.)
- **For:** ~**16%** of world electricity, relatively clean while running, a **low carbon footprint** (the fuel chain's GHG is far below fossil fuels').
- **Against:** ① **every step** of the fuel chain carries risk/pollution/toxic waste (mining → milling/yellowcake → conversion → enrichment → fabrication → reactor → reprocessing); ② worst-case accidents are terrifying — **Three Mile Island 1979 (partial meltdown), Chernobyl 1986 (graphite fire, full loss of control, Pripyat permanently evacuated), Fukushima 2011 (tsunami cut cooling → meltdown, Pacific contamination)**; ③ the waste is dangerous for tens to hundreds of thousands of years with **no long-term solution** (the unverifiability of Yucca Mountain's risk assessment; the failed model at Maxey Flats); ④ **environmental justice (Navajo:** a colonial division of labour, radon progeny driving a lung-cancer relative risk of 28.6, tailings built into homes, RECA); ⑤ **proliferation** (reprocessing yields plutonium; NPT); ⑥ social construction (the "development vs wilderness" discourse around the Ranger mine).

## Discussion 2: E-waste (Week 9)
- **Core:** apply the **7 puzzles** — the digital divide; CRT lead (4–8 lb each) and dioxins; the Basel Convention (**U.S. signed but never ratified**); the **pollution-haven hypothesis**; environmental justice at Guiyu/Agbogbloshie; **the spatial fix + extraction of surplus value**; the agency of informal recyclers.
- **Solutions/critique:** the regulatory contrast (**EU WEEE 4 kg per capita vs no U.S. federal law vs China's regulations**); the market turns waste from an externality into a commodity; consumers should look for **R2/e-Steward certification** and reuse before recycling.

## Discussion 3: Bottled water (Week 12)
- **Core:** apply the **6 puzzles** — **commodification / primitive accumulation** of a free commons; the "two bottles" irony of fairness; the **three types of water scarcity** (hydrological/techno-economic/perceptual); greenwashing / **perceived safety**; the environmental cost of plastic (PET/BPA) + a carbon footprint ~250× that of tap.
- **Signature evidence:** the regulatory asymmetry — **FDA (bottled, loose) vs EPA (tap, strict, SDWA 1974)**; **NRDC 1999** — about 1/3 of bottled water carried significant contamination, "no guarantee it is safer"; **risk-perception bias** (fearing the wrong thing).

## Discussion 4: Fish / tuna (Week 16)
- **Core question:** Is the "dolphin-safe" label a success of green consumption, or greenwashing? Who manages tuna, who benefits, who is harmed? (Apply the 8 puzzles of tuna.)
- **Science:** tuna are warm-blooded migrators (4,000+ miles), family Scombridae; **bioaccumulation of lead and mercury** (Patterson exposed industrial lead pollution; Needleman linked it to children's IQ).
- **Institutions:** **MSY** is the management target, but the high seas are an open commons → a **race to harvest**; the **EEZ (200 nm)** only covers coastal waters, so the high seas need international bodies like **ICCAT**; the U.S. **MMPA 1972** (with the Medina-panel release procedure + a dolphin-mortality cap).
- **Markets:** the **"dolphin-safe" label** — the EII boycott → in 1990 the three big brands (≈90% of the market) committed → dolphin mortality fell ~99%, hailed as a green-consumption success; critique: **MSC certification** thresholds (money/connections) exclude small fishers (post-Fordist).
- **Ethics + construction:** the dolphin is a **charismatic species** (social construction) → **"a victory for dolphins, a defeat for tuna (and the ecosystem)"**: switching to log sets produced **2–3× more bycatch of other species**, or shifted fishing grounds and drove rapid over-exploitation; the **WTO 2012 ruling** found the standard outdated and misleading to consumers.
- **Political economy:** the **2nd contradiction of capitalism** — the more successful the fishing, the fewer fish in the sea; market environmentalism vs political economy.
- **One-line thesis:** the "dolphin-safe" label really did cut dolphin deaths, but it shifted the ecological cost onto other species — green certification solves the problem people watch and hides the problem they don't. (See the §5.2 answer skeleton.)

> **🍟 French fries (Week 15)** — a lecture "object", not one of the four formal discussions, but in the same food module with the same exam logic.
> Core question: is eating fries just a personal choice? Apply the 6 fry puzzles — ① history (Columbian Exchange → the single-variety lesson of the Irish famine) → ② **Russet Burbank**, the perfect fry potato, and high-input agriculture (processed overtook fresh in 1970) → ③ risk (the obesity epidemic + **trans fat**; NYC banned it in 2006, the FDA ruled PHO not GRAS in 2013; "information ≠ better choices") → ④ political economy ("Eat Fries or Else" + marketing/supersizing + the environmental justice of the **food desert**) → ⑤ ethics (biodiversity + **preservation vs conservation** + Leopold's land ethic + the abandoned GM New Leaf). One-line thesis: a single fry exposes "personal dietary choice" as a system shaped jointly by fast-food capital, monoculture agriculture and marketing. (See the §5.3 answer skeleton.)

## Week 2-1 — Urban air pollution & the AQI
1. What is the AQI for? *(→ a single scale that communicates air-pollution levels and health precautions to the public.)*
2. Which law improved U.S. air quality? *(→ the **Clean Air Act**, 1970/1990.)*
3. What role does an atmospheric inversion play in urban air quality? *(→ a warm-over-cold "lid" traps pollutants, blocks dispersion, worsens pollution.)*
4. What are the health effects of air pollution? *(→ irritates/damages the airways; PM2.5 penetrates the alveoli and enters the blood; long exposure raises disease and death.)*
5. In 3–4 sentences, describe the precursors and reactions that produce ozone smog. *(→ NO₂ + hν → NO + O; O + O₂ → O₃; O₃ + NO → NO₂ titration would normally consume O₃; but VOC + NO → NO₂ uses up the NO, slowing titration so O₃ builds up. Precursors = NOₓ + VOCs + sunlight.)*
6. What is particulate matter? Different sizes? Different compositions? Why worry? *(→ yes; yes; the smaller the deeper — PM2.5 reaches the alveoli and blood, composition can include heavy metals/organics, so it is very harmful.)*
7. How should China improve its air quality? What can it learn from the U.S., and vice versa? *(→ open: legislation (Clean-Air-Act-style standards + enforcement), vehicle/coal control, monitoring + disclosure (AQI), regional joint control; conversely China's rapid smog cleanup offers lessons for developing countries.)*

## Week 2-2 — Coal's pollution
1. Lake A pH = 7, Lake B pH = 4 — which has more H⁺ and by how much? *(→ **B, by 10³ = 1000×**; each pH unit down = ×10 H⁺.)*
2. What is bioaccumulation of a toxin? *(→ a toxin concentrates up the food chain and is hard to excrete; the higher the trophic level, the higher the concentration.)*
3. Why should pregnant women worry about fish in their diet? *(→ methylmercury crosses the placenta and harms **fetal neural development**; EPA limits: whole blood < 5 ppb, hair < 1 ppm.)*
4. Name two acids in acid deposition. *(→ sulphuric H₂SO₄, nitric HNO₃.)*
5. What evidence shows coal plants are the main cause of acid precipitation in the eastern U.S.? *(→ concentrated burning of high-sulphur eastern coal + westerlies carrying pollutants to the NE/Canada; sources match downwind deposition.)*
6. Why does mercury in fish rise as the fish gets bigger? *(→ bioaccumulation/biomagnification: big fish live longer, eat more small fish, so methylmercury keeps concentrating.)*
7. What share of U.S. lakes and rivers are under fish-consumption advisories? *(→ use the 1993→2003 doubling of advisories; the share rose substantially.)*
8. Do S and Hg contents differ markedly among U.S. coals? *(→ yes — eastern/interior vs western differ clearly, the east being higher-sulphur.)*

10. As the new U.S. Energy Secretary, how would you supply power while minimising environmental impact? *(→ open: use coal's many harms to argue for efficiency first + a low/zero-carbon mix + emission-control technology.)*
